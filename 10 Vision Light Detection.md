# Chapter 10: Light, Vision, and Detection

Before a character can meaningfully interact with the world, he must first perceive it. Combat, exploration, navigation, stealth, and countless other actions depend upon the ability to see, hear, or otherwise detect surrounding creatures, objects, and events.

The ability to detect things is influenced by distance, lighting, weather, terrain, concealment, movement, noise, and the capabilities of the observer’s senses. Spotting a distant figure is far easier than identifying it clearly, and hearing movement is often possible long before its source can be seen.

The rules below provide general guidelines for illumination, vision, visibility, and audibility under typical adventuring conditions. The DM should adjust these guidelines whenever unusual environments, supernatural effects, or extraordinary circumstances are involved.

## Light and Illumination

Many creatures rely upon light to see clearly. In areas of insufficient illumination, visibility rapidly deteriorates.

Three general lighting conditions are recognized:

- **Bright Light** allows normal vision.
    
- **Dim Light** restricts visibility and may reduce identification or detail ranges.
    
- **Darkness** renders ordinary vision impossible.
- 
Even minimal illumination such as moonlight, distant fires, or phosphorescence may permit limited visibility.

#### Light and Exposure

Light sources allow characters to see, but they also make those characters easier to detect.

Creatures outside an illuminated area may often see the light source and those carrying it before the illuminated characters themselves can clearly distinguish what lies beyond the edge of the light.

Torches, campfires, lanterns, magical illumination, and similar light sources therefore make stealth, ambush, concealment, and inconspicuous travel significantly more difficult.

## Vision

Under ordinary conditions, creatures perceive the world primarily through normal sight. Darkness, concealment, weather, distance, and physical obstruction may all reduce or prevent effective vision.

Certain creatures possess unusual visual capabilities that allow them to perceive their surroundings under conditions impossible for ordinary sight.

### Infravision

Some creatures possess the supernatural sensory ability known as **infravision**.

Infravision allows perception in darkness beyond the capabilities of normal sight. The exact nature of this sense varies between worlds and magical traditions. Some scholars describe it as a physiological sensitivity to unseen emanations or radiations beyond normal vision, while others regard it as an inherently magical form of perception. Regardless of its underlying nature, infravision allows creatures to navigate, detect obstacles, and perceive forms in darkness within its listed range.

Infravision does not function identically to normal sight. Fine colour distinctions, written text, intricate detail, and certain illusions may remain difficult or impossible to perceive through infravision alone at the DM’s discretion.

If not otherwise specified, infravision has a maximum range of 60 feet.

### Using Mirrors

Characters sometimes attempt to observe creatures indirectly through mirrors or reflected surfaces. This is particularly common when confronting creatures whose direct gaze is dangerous, such as a medusa.

A usable reflection requires sufficient illumination to see clearly. Attempting to fight, move, or perform delicate actions while relying entirely upon a reflected image is highly disorienting. Characters attacking or performing actions solely through a mirror suffer a –2 penalty to attack rolls, ability checks, and proficiency checks. In addition, the character loses all Dexterity bonuses to Armour Class against opponents viewed only indirectly through reflections.

### Visibility and Identification

The ability to perceive distant objects depends greatly upon environmental conditions and the amount of detail being observed.

Under ideal conditions on open ground, a moving man-sized figure may be noticed at great distance, though little useful information can be determined. As distance decreases, increasingly specific details become perceptible.

The categories below describe different levels of visual perception:

- **Movement** indicates the maximum distance at which motion may be perceived.
    
- **Detection** indicates the maximum distance at which a stationary or moving figure can be _reliably_ noticed.
    
- **Recognition** indicates the maximum distance at which broad characteristics such as size, species, equipment, or general appearance become distinguishable.
    
- **Identification** indicates the maximum distance at which specific individuals, heraldry, or precise identities may be recognized.
    
- **Detail** indicates the maximum distance at which small actions, facial expressions, or fine manipulation can be clearly observed.

Thus, a creature hidden within dense woods may remain unseen until very close range even on a perfectly clear day.

#### Size and Visibility

Creature size strongly affects visibility. When observing creatures of Size Small, halve all visibility ranges.

Large creatures double their Movement, Detection, and Recognition ranges. Exceptionally large creatures, fortifications, ships, dragons, towers, mountains, fires, or armies may be visible at vastly greater distances.

#### Environmental Conditions

The ranges above assume broadly Earth-like conditions and ordinary atmospheres. Strange worlds, planar environments, magical darkness, supernatural weather, glowing skies, perpetual twilight, or other unusual circumstances may alter visibility dramatically. The DM should freely adjust visibility when environmental conditions warrant it.

## Audibility

The distances at which sounds may be heard depend heavily upon terrain, weather, background noise, elevation, and the listener’s attention.

The ranges below assume relatively open terrain and ordinary ambient conditions.

The DM should freely modify these distances according to conditions. Strong wind, heavy rain, dense forest, urban activity, crowds, or active combat may sharply reduce hearing range. Still air, elevated positions, tunnels, mountain valleys, or open water may greatly increase it.

At night, particularly under calm conditions, sound often seems to carry much farther than during the day. This effect is real enough that distant voices, movement, or activity may sometimes be heard at several times their normal daytime range.

Hearing a sound however does not necessarily reveal its precise source, distance, numbers, or intent.

## Avoiding Detection

Whether stalking prey through dense woodland, slipping unnoticed past castle guards, or avoiding a dangerous threat, creatures often have good reason to avoid detection.

Few creatures are truly invisible or undetectable; most simply exploit circumstances to remain unnoticed, making use of cover, concealment, camouflage, darkness, distraction, or deception.

The rules in this section define cover and concealment, explain how characters hide themselves or others, and conversely how hidden creatures, objects, and hazards may be be discovered. The full suite of thieving skills is also presented here, as most have utility in avoiding detection, whether directly or indirectly.

### **Cover, Concealment, and Masking**

_Cover_ (sometimes called _hard cover_) consists of solid obstacles that physically intervene between an observer and a subject. Stone walls, wooden palisades, closed doors, earthworks, and overturned tables are all examples of cover.

Cover blocks or limits lines of sight and deadens sound. A creature completely behind cover cannot normally be seen, while substantial cover may greatly reduce or entirely block the transmission of sound. Partial cover may leave a creature's presence obvious, but make its identity, actions, or equipment difficult to determine.

_Concealment_ (sometimes called _soft cover_) consists of conditions that obscure a subject without providing substantial physical protection. Darkness, fog, smoke, heavy rain, curtains, foliage, and crowds are all forms of concealment.

Concealment interferes primarily with visual detection. A concealed creature may still be noticed through movement, sound, or other telltale signs, but its precise location, identity, or actions may remain uncertain. Dense concealment may require an observer to actively search before a creature or object can be detected at all.

**Camouflage** is the deliberate creation of **concealment**, allowing creatures or objects to blend into their surroundings through the use of natural or man-made materials. The rules for camouflage are presented under the _Camouflage_ non-weapon proficiency.

_Masking_ consists of conditions or deliberate measures that interfere with the transmission or detection of sound without physically blocking it. Heavy rain, strong winds, rushing water, bustling crowds, and the noise of battle are common forms of environmental masking, while muffling equipment, wrapping armour, or wearing soft-soled footwear are examples of deliberate masking.

Masking interferes primarily with auditory detection. Environmental masking reduces the distance at which sounds may be heard, while deliberate masking reduces the amount of noise a creature produces, making movement and other activity less likely to be noticed.

These forms of protection are cumulative where appropriate. A creature concealed by darkness while sheltering behind a wall during a rainstorm is considerably harder to detect than one relying upon any single form of protection.

The visibility and audibility tables describe the maximum distances at which creatures and sounds may normally be perceived under given environmental conditions. Cover, concealment, and masking do not usually alter these maximum ranges, but instead reduce the likelihood of detection, recognition, or identification within them. Observers may need to move closer, spend time searching, or rely upon other senses before a concealed creature or object is discovered.

#### **Using Cover, Concealment, and Masking**

Cover, concealment, and masking are normally provided by things already in the environment which characters take advantage of through timing and route selection.

## Rogue Skills

Many of the abilities possessed by thieves, bards, ninjas, rangers, and certain other characters concern the avoidance of detection, the discovery of hidden dangers, or the quiet infiltration of guarded places. Although a few abilities, such as **Open Locks** and **Read Languages**, extend beyond the immediate scope of this chapter, they are presented here as they are closely associated with the same style of play.

Each class, class group, or other source that grants rogue skills specifies which skills are available, the character's base chances of success, any special rules governing their use, as well as any discretionary points the character can use to improve those skills.

At character creation begin with the base score for each granted skill and apply the relevant modifiers from Table 59: Rogue Skill Dexterity Adjustments, Table 60: Rogue Skill Racial Adjustments., and Table 62: Rogue Skill Kit Modifiers. If discretionary points are awarded at 1st level, assign these points to skills next. Finally, apply the modifiers for the armour the character is wearing as given in Table 61: Rogue Skill Armour Adjustments. Except for read languages, rogue skills cannot be used while wearing types of armour _not_ listed in Table 61.

Modifiers may reduce a skill below 0%. In such cases, the character must first raise the skill to at least 1% before it can be attempted.

As a character gains experience, additional discretionary points may be awarded or the character's class may provide fixed increases to one or more base skill scores. As at 1st level, thieves and ninjas may assign no more than half these points to a single skill. Also, at the DM’s discretion, some portion of these advancement points may be required to improve skills actively used during recent adventures.

Rogue skills are resolved using percentile dice. Roll d100; if the result is equal to or less than the character's adjusted skill score, the attempt succeeds, except that rolls of 95-100 always fail. The DM should make the roll where the character would be unaware of their success, such as when attempting to hide in shadows. Unless otherwise noted, a skill may be attempted repeatedly, though failure or discovery may carry consequences determined by the circumstances.

#### Pick Pockets

This ability governs filching small objects from another person, palming items unnoticed, concealing small objects upon one's person, and performing simple acts of sleight of hand.

A failed attempt does not necessarily mean the attempt was noticed. To determine whether the victim detects the attempt, subtract three times the victim's level (or HD) from 100. If the Pick Pockets roll equals or exceeds this number, the attempt is noticed. Thus, an inexperienced victim notices only the clumsiest attempts, while seasoned adventurers are much harder to deceive.

At the DM's option, particularly experienced practitioners are themselves more difficult to detect. If the character's level exceeds the victim's, the difference may be added to the detection threshold.

A character may attempt to pick pockets repeatedly, though discovery often carries consequences more severe than simple failure.

#### Open Locks

This ability allows a character to pick conventional locks, manipulate combination mechanisms, and solve puzzle locks or concealed latching systems.

Opening locks normally requires proper thieves' tools or another suitable set of lockpicks. Improvised tools may impose penalties at the DM's discretion depending upon their suitability.

Attempting to open a lock requires 1d10 rounds. A character may attempt to open a particular lock only once per experience level. Failure indicates that the mechanism exceeds the character's current expertise rather than that further attempts are impossible.

#### Find and Remove Traps

This ability enables a character to locate and disable small mechanical traps and alarms such as poisoned needles, spring blades, gas vents, hidden catches, and warning bells.

It does not ordinarily apply to large structural hazards such as collapsing ceilings, moving walls, or massive mechanical devices.

The character must be able to inspect and manipulate the object directly. Searching for a trap requires 1d10 rounds. If a trap is found, the character understands its general function, though not necessarily every detail of its construction.

Disarming a trap also requires 1d10 rounds. Failure usually means the trap cannot presently be defeated. A roll of 96-100 when attempting removal accidentally triggers the trap.

Magical or invisible traps are significantly more difficult to disable; chances of success are typically halved.

#### Move Silently

A character with this ability may attempt to move with exceptional quietness, reducing movement to one-third normal speed. Success is determined secretly by the DM.

Successful silent movement reduces the chance of being detected by hearing and may improve opportunities to surprise opponents or manoeuvre into advantageous positions.

Moving silently provides no benefit if the character remains plainly visible.

#### Hide in Shadows

A character with this ability may conceal himself within shadows, foliage, curtains, alcoves, or similar cover.

This ability functions only when observers are not actively watching the character conceal himself. Once hidden, the character must remain nearly motionless, though small deliberate movements are permitted.

Complete darkness does not aid this ability, as hiding in shadows relies upon camouflage, concealment, and visual misdirection rather than invisibility.

Magical effects capable of revealing hidden or invisible creatures may expose a concealed character.

#### Detect Noise

By remaining still and concentrating for one round, a character with this ability may detect faint noises beyond doors, through passages, or in nearby chambers.

The character must have relative quiet in the immediate area to listen effectively and suffers penalties if wearing a helmet as given in the Helms section of Chapter 8.

The audibility rules presented earlier in this chapter determine whether a sound is **_capable of being heard_** under the prevailing conditions. Detect Noise represents the ability to perceive exceptionally faint or easily overlooked sounds, or to distinguish meaningful sounds from background noise, beyond the ability of an ordinary listener.

#### Climb Walls

Characters with this ability are exceptional climbers, capable of scaling rough surfaces, sheer walls, and even many smooth surfaces without specialized equipment.

While climbing, the character remains vulnerable and is generally unable to fight effectively.

#### Read Languages

Through long exposure to maps, codes, records, and obscure writings, some characters gradually acquire the ability to decipher unfamiliar written languages.

This ability allows the character to interpret nonmagical written material in unknown languages of a culture native to the campaign world. Understanding remains imperfect and depends upon the success of the roll. A successful attempt represents partial or complete comprehension proportional to the character's skill percentage.

Each document requires a separate roll, even if written in the same language. A character may attempt to decipher a given document only once per experience level.

Languages already known through proficiencies require no roll.
