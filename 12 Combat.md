# Chapter 12: Combat

Player Characters are hero-adventurers who delve forgotten dungeons, explore untamed wilderness, and sometimes even find themselves in other planes of existence, standing toe-to-toe with all manner of villains, monsters, and worse. It is inevitable that in overcoming these foes there will be battles and so it is important for all players to know the basic rules for handling combat.

The game would quickly become boring if every encounter was reduced to systematically hacking-and-slashing through opponents so this chapter will cover more than the basic mechanics of hitting or missing, and will present special ways to attack and defend, the use of poisons, rules for turning undead, and more.

### Monster Ability Scores

Many options detailed in this chapter make use of physical ability scores, particularly Strength and Dexterity. As Monster Manual entries do not have stats for monsters, the DM will have to decide whether to generate ability scores for an individual creature, to infer them from other monster stats, or to simply assign them based on best judgment. Suggestions for generating and inferring ability scores for monsters are given in the _Dungeon Master's Guide_.

For player's who are trying to decide whether or not it is worthwhile to attempt a given action against a specific creature, however a very rough guideline is that greater size typically equates to greater strength and constitution, while faster movement often suggests higher dexterity.

## The Combat Round

If an encounter escalates (or devolves) into combat, the time scale of the game automatically goes to rounds. Rounds are used to measure the actions of characters in combat (and in other circumstances where timing is important).

A round is approximately one minute long in-game. Ten combat rounds equal a turn (e.g. about 10 minutes of game time). This is particularly important to remember for spells that last for turns, rather than rounds. These are of course just approximations--precise time measurements are impossible to make in combat. Something that takes only a few moments to accomplish under normal circumstances could become an undertaking of heroic scale when attempted in the middle of the chaos of battle.

There is no subdivision of the round into smaller discrete units of time, but what are sometimes called segments are used to establish the order in which events unfold within a round. Weapon speeds, casting times of spells that are less than 1 round, etc. are common examples. They are relative amounts of time rather than a specific number of seconds.

### What You Can Do in One Round

A character can accomplish **one basic action within a combat round**. The round represents approximately a minute of chaotic back-and-forth activity, not a single motion, and each basic action - whether attacking, casting a spell, drinking a potion, or something else - includes incidental actions that do not _need_ to be described or adjudicated.

For example a melee attack is not simply a swing of a sword, it represents the flurry of testing strikes, feints, parries, and footwork as the combatants seek an opening to exploit for a more telling blow. The dice are adjudicating the _overall outcome_ of these efforts. When characters gain the ability to make multiple attacks per round, it is not because they are suddenly moving faster but because they are better able to create opportunities within that same span of frantic activity.

Likewise even a 'simple' task such as quaffing a potion involves more than just lifting a bottle. In the heat of battle, the character must keep their footing, free a hand, rummage through their pack, uncork and finally swallow a potion, all while fending off threats, avoiding strikes, and withstanding spells.

A combat round therefore contains about a full minute of effort, which the rules reduce to one basic action **plus** whatever **incidental movements and reactions** occur within that time.

Some examples of what can be done in a round:

- Make an attack (may involve multiple attack rolls)
    
- Cast a spell (if the casting time is less than 1 round)
    
- Use a magical item
    
- Drink a potion
    
- Light a torch
    
- Bind a wound
    
- Search a body
    
- Recover a dropped weapon
    

In addition there are actions that take a negligible amount of time that a character may do without affecting their ability to perform a more important task, examples include:

- shout brief warnings, demands, or commands (but not carry on full conversations)
    
- drop a weapon and draw another
    
- Shed excess equipment such as backpacks

### The Surprise Round

A **Surprise Round** represents the brief instant in which alert combatants react before those caught unprepared can respond. Unlike a normal combat round, it does not represent a full minute of combat, but only the first few moments of an unexpected attack.

**A surprised creature** can take no action during the Surprise Round unless another rule specifically allows it. A surprised creature also loses any Dexterity bonus to Armour Class for the duration of the Surprise Round.

For creatures that are not surprised, the Surprise Round is resolved using the normal combat sequence, except that the following limitations apply:

- A creature may make no more than **two attack rolls** during the Surprise Round, regardless of its normal number of attacks or rate of fire.
    
- Only spells with a casting time of **1** may be cast.
    
- Spells requiring a casting time greater than **1**, or measured in rounds or turns, cannot be begun during a Surprise Round. If such a spell was already being cast, the Surprise Round does not count towards its casting time.
    
- Opportunity attacks may be made normally if triggered.
    

If every combatant is surprised, or no combatant is surprised, no Surprise Round occurs and combat proceeds normally.

If a creature possesses an ability allowing it to act despite being surprised, that ability is resolved during the Surprise Round as described by the relevant rule.

## The Combat Sequence

In real life, combat is close to pure anarchy, filled with confusion and uncertainty. To play out a battle in a game however it is necessary to impose some order. Consequently, within a combat round there are a fixed series of steps that must be followed:

1. DM Action Determination
    
2. Player Action Declaration
    
3. Determine Initiative
    
4. Resolution of Actions
    
5. Resolution of Round
    
#### Step 1. DM Action Determination

The round begins with the DM secretly deciding the actions that will be taken (including specific spells to be cast, if any) by all characters under their control. The DM is on their honour not to change actions or spells after learning the player' intended actions - after all, the Monsters/NPCs don't know what the PCs are going to do. If necessary, the DM should make brief notes.

In choosing these actions the DM will normally choose whatever is likely to be the most advantageous/sensible for the situation and type of creature, for example creatures that benefit from swarming an opponent will likely try to do so, creatures that are severely injured will likely try to withdraw or flee, if reinforcements are nearby they will try to alert them if they are being overwhelmed, etc.

#### Step 2. Player Action Declaration

Once the DM has made their decision, they will ask the players to state (broadly) what their characters will be attempting to do, including specific spells to be cast (if any). At this point the players are _usually_ free to informally discuss amongst themselves, coordinating tactics, planning movement and positioning, revising battle priorities, and may also ask the DM for rule clarifications. The DM may however limit or entirely restrict this discussion (except for rule clarifications) to reflect applicable in-game conditions such as magical silence and surprise.

Players are expected to keep this discussion as brief as is necessary to decide what their individual characters will try to do (and if the discussion is going in circles, increasing decision paralysis rather than reducing it, or otherwise taking an unreasonable amount of time, the DM may tell the players to wrap it up) . At the end of this discussion each player declares what action their character will take. **Actions that must be declared** include:

- Attacks, indicating which weapon(s), or type of unarmed attack
    
- Covering
    
- Guarding
    
- Holding
    
- Parrying
    
- Special manoeuvres (called shots, grappling, backstabs, etc.) Note: some manoeuvres are **not** declared, refer to their descriptions
    
- Special Types of movement (withdrawing, charging, running)
    
- Casting a spell, indicating the specific spell
    
- Using an Item (potions, wands, scrolls, etc.)
    
- Using a mystical power, indicating which is being used
    
- Turning Undead

These actions are described in more detail throughout this chapter.

#### Step 3. Determine Initiative

Once all players have declared their actions, the DM will call for initiative to be rolled. This determines the order in which the declared actions will happen within the round. The DM should specify which method of initiative the players are to use (a table rule may define one method as a default), who is making the roll in the case of the group or modified group initiative methods, and indicate any group initiative modifiers that apply to the roll.

With the individual and modified group initiative methods each player is responsible for applying all applicable modifiers and keeping track of their initiative for the round. With the group initiative method the player that is called on to make the roll is responsible for applying the group initiative modifier to the roll and keeping track of the final value.

The methods and modifiers are explained in more detail under the heading Initiative below.

#### Step 4. Resolution of Actions

With initiative established, the next step is to determine the success or failure of each combatant's actions. The DM will call on the player to describe what their character does. In response to changing battle conditions, and subject to the rules given later, players may choose to abandon, delay (hold), or change a declared action, and should indicate this immediately when called on. If the player is continuing with their declared action they should describe the path of any movement (or if applicable move their token along its path) and detail their characters action. Players are encouraged to sprinkle some so-to-speak 'narrative flair' to their description if they are comfortable doing so, but are not required to. The DM will interject as needed to call for any rolls.

#### Step 5. Resolution of Round

After all actions for the round have been resolved, the DM handles any remaining effects and administrative tasks. These end-of-round checks ensure that ongoing conditions and time-based effects are applied correctly. The DM resolves them in whatever order is most convenient, but the following elements are typically addressed:

1. **Update Status Effects**
   
This includes:
    
- Applying continuing effects of spells cast in previous rounds whose durations have not expired.
    
- Resolving spells with a casting time of one full round or more, if their casting finishes this round.
    
- Applying ongoing or delayed damage (poison, acid, fire, bleeding, etc.).
    
- Confirming or updating the conditions of characters: knocked down, stunned, grappled, bound, slowed, and similar states.

2. **Repositioning Due to Forced-Movement**  
    Characters who were forced to retreat, pushed, knocked back, or displaced are shifted to their final positions on the battlefield.
    
3. **Determine Fatigue**  
    If the optional fatigue rules are used, the DM checks whether any character or creature becomes fatigued this round due to exertion, environment, or special abilities.
    
4. **Morale Checks (NPCs and Monsters)**  
    The DM makes morale checks as required by the situation—typically when significant losses occur, leaders fall, or other shock events unfold.

Once these tasks are complete, the battlefield state is fully updated and another combat round begins with **Step 1: DM Action Determination**.

## Movement and Position

Before delving into the actions characters can take in combat, it is necessary to explain how and when characters can move in battle, and the effects of where and how they are positioned.

### Movement in Combat

#### Movement Rate

As discussed in the Adventuring chapter, a character’s base movement rate is determined by their race or species. For playable races, this value is given in the racial descriptions in Chapter 4; for monsters, it appears in their stat block. To recap, the most common base movement rates for playable races are **12** for Medium creatures and **6** for Small creatures. These base values may be increased or decreased by ability scores, class or kit features, spell effects, injuries, encumbrance, and other factors.

In combat, a character’s movement rate is equal to the number of squares the character may traverse on a standard battle map (a 5-foot grid) in a full round (1 minute) of movement. This represents the cautious, tactical movement demanded by battle conditions: assessing obstacles, scanning for threats, and looking for opportunities to exploit.

#### Dividing Movement

Movement is an allowance for the entire round and, with certain exceptions, may be divided and expended before taking an action, between attacks with the same weapon, or after taking an action, so long as the total movement allowance for the round is not exceeded. All movement must be taken when the character has initiative to act (see _Initiative_, below).

While movement in reality has a time component that would affect the order in which actions occur and where creatures are when effects take place, to reduce bookkeeping and simplify play, movement is treated as though it occurs instantaneously while the character has initiative to act. No voluntary movement occurs outside a character’s initiative unless another rule specifically allows it.

When characters act simultaneously due to an initiative tie, it is the **action** that occurs simultaneously. Any movement taken before the action is completed for all such characters first, then actions are resolved with characters at the locations where they perform those actions, followed by any remaining movement.

If a character has multiple attacks with the same weapon, they may divide their movement freely between those attacks, moving before an attack, after an attack, or both, so long as their total movement for the round is not exceeded.

#### Shifting

Some actions, such as spellcasting, restrict movement for the round or operate by different rules when combined with movement (for example, rate of fire for missile attacks). For the purposes of such rules, a character may **shift** to an adjacent grid square on their initiative count, immediately before their action is resolved; this shift **does not count as movement**.

A shift represents a minor adjustment in position, such as stepping to maintain or regain a line of fire. For example, an archer may shift one square to bring a target back into view without affecting their rate of fire.

If a shift causes the character to become visible to an enemy that did not previously have line of sight to them (for example, the character had **greater than 90% cover or concealment** from that enemy), and that enemy has at least one **ranged or thrown weapon attack remaining** for the round, the enemy may immediately resolve **one** such attack against the shifting character. This attack is resolved **before** the shifting character’s action and may disrupt spellcasting or other actions as normal.

A shift may also provoke **attacks of opportunity** as described elsewhere, if the normal conditions for such attacks are met.

### Specific Types of Movement

The following types of combat movement must be declared before initiative is rolled. When performing one of these movement types, the character’s movement **may not be divided**, and any unused portion of the movement allowance is lost. For example, a character cannot complete a charge and then use remaining movement afterward, nor partially withdraw and then move normally.

#### Charging

Charging is rapid movement across the battlefield intended to end with an attack. A charging character may move up to **one and one-half times** their movement rate and make a single attack. The momentum of the charge grants a **+2 bonus** to this attack. Some weapons inflict double damage when used in a charge, as noted in the Master Weapons Table.

A charging character however also suffers a **+2 penalty to initiative**, loses any Dexterity bonus to armour Class, and incurs an additional **+1 penalty to AC**. In addition, they suffer **double damage** from defenders who are set to receive a charge and make a successful attack.

#### Running and Sprinting

In combat, running allows a character to double their movement but, except for tackles, prevents them from taking any other action in the round, including making opportunity attacks. It is also dangerous to move rapidly through a battlefield: while running, as with charging, a character loses any Dexterity bonus to armour Class and suffers an additional **+1 penalty to AC**. Further, if they run into the threatened area of a creature that is set against a charge, they suffer the same doubled damage as if they had been charging.

If a character has declared that they are running, on their initiative count they may attempt to **sprint** instead, achieving greater speed. To sprint, they must make a Strength check. If successful, their movement is tripled instead of doubled. They may then choose to attempt a second Strength check at a **–4 penalty** to further increase their speed. If this second check succeeds, their movement is quadrupled, and they may attempt a final Strength check at a **–8 penalty** to achieve quintuple speed. If any Strength check fails, the character does not increase their speed further but may continue moving at the speed already achieved.

At the end of a round of sprinting, the character must make a Constitution check with a penalty based on the **highest speed reached during that round**: **–1 for triple speed, –2 for quadruple speed, or –3 for quintuple speed**. If the check fails, the character is exhausted and may move only at half their normal movement rate in the following round.

If the check succeeds, the character may continue sprinting in the next round at the same speed or at a lower speed without rolling additional Strength checks. If they are not already moving at quintuple speed and have not failed any Strength checks during the current run, they may also attempt to increase their speed using the same rules as above.

At the end of each subsequent round of running or sprinting, a Constitution check must be made. **The penalties from each round of sprinting during the current run are cumulative**. Slowing to a lower speed can reduce or prevent additional penalties from being added for that round but does not remove penalties already accumulated. The accumulated penalty persists until the run ends (i.e., the character spends one full round moving at normal speed or less).

#### Withdrawing

Withdrawing is the only safe way to leave a square threatened by an opponent, carefully backing away while keeping one’s face toward the enemy and one’s guard up. A withdrawing character may move up to **half their movement rate** and does not provoke opportunity attacks from enemies they are aware of in their front or flanks (see _Facing_, below), but may still provoke attacks from unseen enemies or those to their rear.

A character who is withdrawing cannot attack, cast spells, or use most items during that round, but may still make opportunity attacks.

Because an opponent may, on their own initiative, choose to follow a withdrawing character in order to keep them threatened, withdrawal is most effective when the withdrawing character acts on a later initiative count than their enemy or when an ally also threatens the same opponent, impeding pursuit.

The alternative way to retreat from combat is fleeing, which is simply running for a specific purpose and follows the normal rules for running.

### Positioning

#### Stance (Kneeling, Sitting, and Prone)

While most fighting is done on one's feet, character's may adopt - or be forced into - different positions. As a generalization a position closer to the ground decreases the risk of being hit by ranged attacks, but simultaneously increases the risk of being struck by melee attacks.

A **kneeling** character gains a -1 bonus to AC against missile-like attacks, but a melee attack against them is made with a +2 bonus. A character may use any weapon without penalty while kneeling.

A **sitting** character has the same bonuses and penalties as a kneeling character, but can only use a crossbow, blowgun or small-size firearm (if available) without penalty from this position. All other weapons incur a -2 penalty to attack. (note that a rider is mounted, _not_ sitting)

A **prone** character gains a -2 bonus to AC against missile-like attacks, but melee attacks against them are made with a +4 bonus. A prone character may only use crossbows and firearms (again, if available) at 1/2 the usual rate of fire, blowguns, or small-sized melee weapons. A prone character attempting to make a melee attack suffers a -4 penalty to hit, thrown weapons do not suffer this penalty to attack.

A character may drop into a lower position as a free action. Rising from prone to kneeling/sitting, or from kneeling/sitting to standing increases the characters initiative by 2 and imposes a -2 penalty to any subsequent attacks made in the round. Rising directly from prone to standing requires a full round - the character can do nothing else.

A character may not benefit simultaneously from both positional modifiers (kneeling, sitting, prone) and cover. If a character is covered, the cover modifier applies and their position is irrelevant for purposes of defense.

A character’s position may, however, determine whether they can claim cover in the first place (for example, kneeling behind a low wall). If cover is later lost—for instance, if an opponent moves to bypass or negate the cover—then the character’s positional modifiers immediately apply once again.

#### Facing

Facing is the direction a character is oriented in during combat, defining their front, flanks, and rear. It determines what they can see and how effectively they can respond to attacks from various directions. A character’s **front** is the arc within their field of vision, the **flanks** are the areas at the edge of that vision, and the **rear** is their blind spot.

For Medium or smaller creatures when using a square grid battle map, the **front** consists of the three squares directly ahead of the character (the square they face and one to either side). The **rear** is the corresponding three squares directly opposite the front. The **flanks** are the remaining square on each side. Larger creatures occupy more grid squares, and the number of squares in each arc increases proportionally with their size and shape. A number of unusual creatures, for example jellies and otyughs, do not have an identifiable 'front' flanks or rears and all areas are treated as front.

Unless a player specifies otherwise, characters are assumed to face the enemy they are currently engaging, or—if not actively fighting—the nearest creature they are aware of. When threatened by multiple foes, it is similarly assumed that the character adjusts their facing to minimize the number of enemies in their rear arc and maximize those in their front, with the caveat that an enemy they are currently engaging must remain in their front arc or their attack is lost. When moving characters are assumed to face in the direction of movement, except when withdrawing. Players may opt to specify a different facing for their character, remembering they cannot engage a creature that is not to their front. Characters may change facing immediately when they first become aware of a creature that threatens them, when a creature they are aware of ceases to threaten them, or on their initiative count.

#### Reach

Reach is the distance at which a creature can make a melee attack against an opponent. It is determined by the weapon wielded, if any, and the creature’s size.

A weapon’s reach is given in the Master Weapons Table, expressed as a number of grid squares on a 5-foot battle map. Weapons with a reach of 1 (5 feet) may attack only creatures in squares immediately adjacent to the wielder.

Weapons with a reach greater than 1 and which deal slashing and/or bludgeoning damage may attack any square within their reach. Weapons that deal _only piercing damage_, however, may attack **only at the maximum extent of their reach**, and not at nearer squares. Such weapons (including lances and pikes) are designed and balanced for thrusting with the point and cannot be effectively used to strike adjacent foes.

A monster’s natural attacks are assumed to have a base weapon reach of 1 (5 feet) unless the creature’s description suggests otherwise.  
The reach of a creature’s weapon, _including natural weapons_, is modified by the creature’s size. Large creatures increase weapon reach by 1 square, Huge creatures by 2 squares, and Gargantuan creatures by 3 squares.

Creatures normally cannot make melee attacks **through an occupied square** to strike an enemy farther away, regardless of their reach. An intervening creature is assumed to block swings, limit space, and interfere with timing. Weapons that deal only piercing damage are an exception and may be thrust through an occupied square to attack at the maximum extent of it's reach unless the DM rules the intervening creature completely blocks the line of attack. Another exception is creatures fighting in a formation, who may make melee attacks through a square occupied by an ally as it is assumed that their movements are coordinated to allow

Other exceptional circumstances may allow attacks through an occupied square at the DM’s discretion. For example, a monster’s tentacles might strike over or around an intervening foe, or a character swinging a polearm might be able to attack over a smaller creature to strike a much larger opponent.

#### Threatening

The area where a character can potentially make attacks is the area that creature 'threatens'. This area is determined by reach, and facing (and thus _indirectly_ by creature size). A creature only threatens the squares that are to their front and within reach of their attack. Remember however, as explained in the facing rules above, that a creature is assumed to adjust their facing based on the threats they are aware of - a creature that has no threats nearby consequently threatens all squares in reach for any enemy it is aware of, while a creature currently engaged against an opponent, must keep that opponent in their front or lose their attack (and possibly trigger an opportunity attack by their change in facing as described later.)

#### Flanking

Flanking is an attempt to exploit an opponent's facing, whether it is multiple attackers trying to position themselves so that at least one attacker is either to the rear or a flank, or by using surprise, stealth, and subterfuge to sneak up behind the target unseen.

Attacks from a flank gain a +1 bonus to hit. Attacks from the rear gain a +2 bonus to hit _and_ the target's bonuses to AC from dexterity or a shield (unless the shield is slung on the character's back) do not apply to an attack from the rear.

#### Cover and Concealment

An effective tactic for avoiding missile fire and some spell effects is to hide behind obstacles—commonly referred to as **taking cover**. _Cover_ (sometimes called _hard cover_) consists of solid obstacles that physically block attacks, while _concealment_ (or _soft cover_) obscures the target without providing substantial physical protection. Stone walls, wooden palisades, and overturned tables are examples of cover; curtains, fog, smoke, and bushes are examples of concealment.

When a creature is targeted by a missile attack or an applicable spell, the DM considers the nature, size, density, and placement of all intervening obstacles (including creatures*) that the line of fire—a straight line from the center of the attacker to the center of the target—passes through. The DM also considers the size, stance, and awareness of the target; creatures with an Intelligence score lower than 3 will rarely make effective use of cover.

Based on these factors, the DM determines whether the obstacles collectively provide cover or concealment, and how much of the target is hidden from that attacker.

For missile attacks, the value from the table below is applied as a **penalty to the attack roll**. For **hard cover only**, the same value is applied as a **bonus to saving throws** against spells that cause physical damage. In addition, a character who is at least **90% behind cover** suffers **half damage on a failed saving throw** and **no damage on a successful saving throw** against such effects.

**Intervening creatures typically provide concealment rather than hard cover**, unless they are significantly larger than the target or are deliberately trying to create cover for those behind them (for example forming a shield wall).

### Opportunity Attacks

Some things a character does can create an opportunity for an opponent to immediately attack them. These attacks of opportunity are mentioned here as they are frequently triggered by movement and/or positioning, and thus important in deciding what movements a character will make.

An opportunity attack is a single free melee _strike_ immediately made against a threatened creature - if a creature normally makes multiple strikes in a single attack (such as character wielding two weapons, or monsters who have a claw/claw/bite attack routine), they must choose which is being used before making an attack roll. It is 'free' because it is made _in addition to_ any actions the threatening character has declared for the current round - it does not replace those actions.

There is no specific limit to the number of opportunity attacks a character may suffer in a turn - but a creature cannot make more than one attack of opportunity against the same target in the same round. There _is_ also a limit to the total number of opportunity attacks a character can make in a single round. Warriors and monsters can make 3 attacks of opportunity, while other characters can only make 1. All creatures gain 1 additional opportunity attack for every 5 levels or hit dice. (a 20th level warrior can thus make up to 7 attacks of opportunity _each round_, while one of the most formidable monsters known to exist - the Tarrasque - given sufficient targets and opportunities could make up to 17 per round).

The following actions by a threatened creature create an opportunity to attack:

- Voluntarily moving away from the threatening creature, unless the action declared was a 'withdrawal'
    
- A change in facing that results in the threatening creature now being to the rear
    
- Attempting to fire a missile weapon at a target other than the threatening creature.
    
- Attempting an unarmed attack while threatened by any foe that is not another unarmed humanoid.
    
Some actions that fall into these categories may not be obvious (for example mounted characters attempting to trample enemies is considered a form of overbearing which is considered an unarmed attack) - and others may modify or limit these triggers; read the description of an action to determine whether, and under what circumstances, it triggers an opportunity attack.

In addition to the effects described above, a character who is attempting to bypass an enemy and incurs an attack of opportunity as they move away has their movement stopped **if** the result of the opportunity attack is a successful critical hit or a knockdown, otherwise the character may complete their movement.

## Actions

### Attack

Attacking is, perhaps unsurprisingly, the most common action of combatants. When you declare an attack you must indicate **which weapon** will be used because the individual weapon's speed is a modifier to initiative. Each type of attack (Melee, Missile-like, Non-Lethal, etc) has its own rules, but all resolve with the same mechanic: the attack roll.

#### The Attack Roll

The basic mechanic of the attack roll is that each character has an effective THAC0, the number they must roll on 1d20 in order **To Hit armour Class 0**. To determine the number needed to hit any other armour class you simply subtract target's AC from the attacker' effective THAC0.

A character's effective THAC0 begins with their base THAC0 (determined by class and level) you then _subtract_ all applicable modifiers. Since subtracting a negative number is equivalent to adding, penalties (negative modifiers) are _added_, while bonuses (positive modifiers) are _subtracted_.

For example, a level 7 Fighter has a base THAC0 of 14. His strength of 18 gives a +1 bonus to hit, the magical +2 sword he is using gives another bonus to hit, and before battle the cleric cast bless on the party granting an additional +1 bonus to attack rolls, for a total modifier of +4. His effective THAC0 for the attack is thus 10 (e.g. 14 base minus +4 modifier)

When the player is asked to make an attack roll, they roll 1d20, and subtract the result from their effective THAC0. This gives the lowest (best) armour class that is hit with that roll.

For example, our fighter above rolls the d20 and the result is 7. With his effective THAC0 of 10 this roll will hit any target with an armour class of 3 (e.g. 10-7) or worse (i.e. higher than 3)

If there are modifiers the player is unaware of - for example they are unknowingly wielding a cursed weapon or affected by a spell - the DM applies these secretly. The armour Class the player announced is what they _believe_ they hit and the DM compares this against the target's _effective_ AC after adjusting for any unknown modifiers.

For example, our fighter's player announces that he hits armour class 3. The target is wearing Elven Chain Mail, +1 (AC 4), and they have a dexterity adjustment of -1 giving an actual AC of 3. The target has however also cast _displace self_ which imposes a -2 penalty on the attacker (after the first attack which automatically missed). The DM applies that penalty by improving the target's AC to an effective AC of 1. The DM announces that the attack misses.

#### Impossible To-Hit Numbers

Sometimes the attacker’s to-hit number seems impossible to roll, or conversely impossible to _not_ roll. In either case, a roll is still required. This is because modifiers—especially those known only to the DM—may change the outcome in ways the attacker cannot perceive.

Further, unless the DM rules otherwise, a natural roll of 20 is _always_ a hit, and a natural roll of 1 is _always_ a miss. The most unskilled combatant can get lucky, but even a natural 20 cannot make a mundane weapon strike a creature that is inherently immune to it, such as an ethereal or incorporeal being.

#### Critical Hits

Fantasy literature and film is full of decisive battles where a single brutal blow against an enemy can turn the tide - a limb severed, a skull cleaved. Critical hits fill this role in the game, not for the purpose of adding sickening violence, but by adding a small chance of specific injuries that help to make combat more than a simple question of who runs out of hit points first.

A **critical hit** occurs on a natural roll of 18 or higher **if** the roll hits by at least 5.

For example, the fighter discussed previously with an effective THAC0 of 10 rolls a 19, he thus hits any armour class of -9 or worse. If the target's _effective_ armour class (after all modifiers) is worse than -4 he has scored a critical hit.

When a critical hit is scored _with a weapon_, the **base weapon damage is doubled**, or if the weapon is two or more sizes larger than the target, tripled. If the damage is already multiplied for another reason only the base weapon damage is doubled (in effect the multiplier is increased). Modifiers (strength, magical bonuses, etc.) are then added to the result. For example a light horse lance ordinarily does 1d6 damage, used while charging the rolled damage is doubled, if the attack roll results in a critical hit the rolled damage is now tripled, unless the target is smaller than size M (i.e. the lance at size L is then two or more sizes larger than the target) in which case the rolled damage is quadrupled.

If the target survives this damage they must then make a **saving throw vs death**. If the target fails this save they _may_ have a specific injury which further impairs them in some manner, depending on the location and severity of the strike.

The location struck is normally determined by rolling 1d10 and consulting the table below. The exceptions are a) where the attack was a called shot, in which case the location is wherever the attacker had aimed, b) if the attacker is fighting a creature 2 or more sizes larger than themselves roll 1d6 (less likely to hit head and upper torso), c) conversely if the attacker is fighting a creature 2 or more sizes smaller than themselves roll 1d6+4 (less likely to strike legs).

| Roll | Humanoid  | Animals and Monsters    |
| ---- | --------- | ----------------------- |
| 1    | Right Leg | Right Foreleg/Claw/Wing |
| 2    | Right Leg | Left Foreleg/Claw/Wing  |
| 3    | Left Leg  | Right Hind Leg          |
| 4    | Left Leg  | Left Hind Leg           |
| 5    | Abdomen   | Tail*                   |
| 6    | Torso     | Abdomen                 |
| 7    | Torso     | Abdomen                 |
| 8    | Right Arm | Torso/Chest             |
| 9    | Left Arm  | Torso/Chest             |
| 10   | Head      | Head                    |

\* for fish- and snake-like creatures (i.e. do not have legs) treat rolls of 1-5 as tail.

The damage inflicted by the attack, tempered by the resilience of the target, affect what dice are rolled to determine the specific effect. The _potential_ severity is determined by dividing the total damage inflicted by the critical hit (the multiplied weapon damage plus all applicable modifiers) by 1/4 of the target's level or HD (rounded up) and consulting the following table:

|Damage / 1/4 target's HD|Potential Severity|Dice Rolled|
|---|---|---|
|0-4|Minor|1d6|
|5-6|Major|2d4|
|7-8|Severe|2d6|
|9+|Mortal|2d8|

High damage critical hits against weaker enemies are consequently more likely to result in mortal injuries (if the critical damage does not kill the target outright); inflicting a mortal injury on a Dragon that has survived to Great Wyrm age however is only achievable by the greatest of warriors, and even then requires a great deal of luck.

After the location and severity have been determined the DM will consult the appropriate tables for the type of damage (bludgeoning/piercing/slashing) and type of target (humanoid/animal/monster) to determine what specific injuries were inflicted (if any).

#### Types of Attacks

**Melee Attack:** an attack with a weapon (including natural weapons such as claws) at hand-to-hand range.

**Missile-like Attack:** attacks made at distance, with direct-fire, single target missiles such as quarrels, arrows, darts, and thrown weapons

**Grenade-Like Attack:** attacks made at distance that have an area effect, such as flasks of poison, oil, holy water, and boulders

**Non-Lethal Attack:** Includes overbearing, punching, wrestling, martial arts, and using weapons in a non-lethal manner

**Touch Spell Attack:** Although they are a type of attack, these are declared as 'Casting a Spell' and are covered in that section

#### Melee Attacks

Melee attacks are arguably the most basic form of attack, the attacker attempts to strike an opponent that is within the reach of the weapon(s) in their hand(s). The attacker makes an attack roll as described above and if it is successful they roll for damage, applying any relevant damage modifiers.

If the attacker has **multiple weapons**, all the attacks are resolved on their initiative - for example many beasts will make 2 claw attacks and a bite attack, and some characters will choose to wield 2 weapons. If the attacker has **multiple attacks** (i.e. with the same weapon) the first attack occurs on their initiative, and the subsequent attack(s) occur after all combatants have made their first attacks. If more than one combatant has multiple attacks they are cycled through in initiative order, one attack at a time.

#### Missile-Like Attacks

Missile combat involves everything that melee combat does, but there are a few additional rules that apply only to missile combat.

First, the **range** of the target must be determined. If the range is greater than the long range of the weapon (see master weapons list) the target cannot be hit. If the distance is between the medium and long range, the target is at **long range** and the attack suffers a **-5 penalty**. If the distance is between the short and medium range, the target is at **medium range** and the attack suffers a **-2 penalty**. Distances less than the short range of the weapon have no penalty. Some weapons have no short range and always incur penalties.

Second, missile-like weapons have a **rate of fire**. Some are quick and can be fired/thrown multiple times per round while others are slow and can only be used every other round. If the weapon can be fired multiple times in a round, it is treated the same as a melee weapon with multiple attacks, with the first attack resolved on the attacker's initiative, and subsequent attacks occur after all primary attacks have been resolved.

Third, missile-like weapons are really meant for engaging enemies _before_ they reach your lines. Once steel begins meeting steel in melee there is a risk that an ally will choose an inopportune moment to dodge in the path your missile is travelling. When missile-like weapons are **fired into melee** the DM counts the number of figures in the immediate area of the target (i.e. threatened or threatening the target), counting small creatures as 1/2, medium creatures as 1, large creatures as 2, huge creatures as 4 and gargantuan creatures as 6. The ratio of a figure's size, to the total, is the chance that they will be the actual target. For example if an ally is in single combat with an enemy of the same size, there is a 50% chance of hitting your ally.

#### Grenade-like Attacks

Grenade-like attacks resemble missile-like attacks, except that the rate of fire 1/rd, the DM determines the range based on the weight of the object thrown, and the target can simply be a point on the ground. As a guideline objects less than 5 pounds can be thrown up to 30' with medium range of 20' and short range of 10'. Exceptionally heavy objects can only be thrown with a successful bend bar/lift gates (Strength) check. An object cannot be thrown if it exceeds the characters max press.

Most grenade-like attacks involve throwing a container that contains some sort of substance that actually causes the effect. In most cases a container breaks immediately when it lands, however there may be doubt about more durable containers (a leather flask or hard pottery). In such cases an item saving throw is made (DM has the relevant info on this) to see if the container shatters, rips, or otherwise spews its contents.

Grenade-like attacks that miss the intended target still function (if the container breaks), and the DM has information on resolving these situations.

##### Notes on Specific Types of Grenade-like Attacks

**Acid** - acids are very rare, but very useful against creatures like trolls as acid damage cannot be healed by regeneration.

**Holy Water** - affects most types of undead and creatures from the lower planes, but has no effect on creatures that have no material form or are in a gaseous form. Similar to acid, damage from holy water cannot be regenerated. Unholy water (e.g. an evil priest's holy water) operates the same except that it affects paladins, creatures from the upper planes, and some creatures whose purpose is to defend good (lammasu, shedu, etc.)

**Oil** - Oil only causes damage when set ablaze. This normally involves a two-step process of soaking the target in oil, and then lighting it, and thus it often requires two-attack rolls. Flaming oil burns for 2 rounds, causing 2d6 damage in the first round and 1d6 damage in the second.

**Poison** - Few poisons are capable of being used in a grenade-like attack (most need to be injected or ingested) but some contact poisons may work.

### Called Shot

A called shot is an attack carefully aimed at a specific location on a target. It can be used to strike an object the target is holding (for example to destroy the potion they were about to imbibe), to aim for a more vulnerable part of the target (an unarmoured head or face), or with the intention of causing a specific effect (blinding an enemy by attacking their eye). They may also be used when firing missiles into melee to eliminate the risk of striking an ally. Some creatures, such as slimes and oozes, have no discernible vital locations to target with a called shot; Unless the DM rules otherwise, the only benefit of making one against such creatures is ensuring allies are not struck by stray missiles.

When a called shot is declared, the specific point of aim and/or intended effect must be given, and the added care taken in aiming imposes an initiative penalty of +1. If a character suffers any effect that disrupts their aim (knocked down, grappled, etc) before their initiative, the called shot reverts to a normal attack against the target (if the player is still able to take an action).

By default called shots present the attacker with a -4 penalty on the attack roll, but the DM may modify this to reflect the circumstances; if the target is surprised and the point of aim is relatively large, there might be no penalty whereas attempting to stab an enemy through the eyeslit of their great helm during melee might inflict a penalty of -8 on the attack. If using a called shot to avoid hitting allies in melee, the attack does not risk striking an unintended target, but the called shot penalty always applies and cannot be less than -4 for this purpose. This penalty is not applied to the attack if the called shot is disrupted.

As detailed in the section on critical hits, if a called shot results in a critical hit that may cause a specific injury, the location of that injury is determined by where the critical hit was aimed. See that section for full details.

#### Special Uses of Called Shots

The rules of called shots are an appropriate basis for adjudicating many other non-standard actions a character might wish to attempt. Some examples inspired by film and fiction include:

- **Carving initials.** A called shot may be used with an appropriate type of weapon to carve or slash a single letter onto a suitable medium - a door, table, wall, an opponent's clothing or skin. If the target is a creature this inflicts only 1 point of damage, but may cause a weaker enemy to lose morale or surrender. On the other hand, a stronger enemy is likely to take this insult extremely personally and may fixate on exacting revenge.
    
- **Cutting Off Accoutrements.** A called shot with an appropriate type of weapon might be used to cut off or detach buttons, medals, brooches, or braces (suspenders) from an opponent’s clothing; to cut a carrying strap or purse string; to slip a necklace over someone’s head; or to perform some other act requiring precision. The narrative effect of such actions is determined by the DM on a case-by-case basis, but as a general rule the mechanical effect should be a penalty to morale that lasts for the remainder of the encounter; cutting off additional accoutrements does not normally produce additional effect. Such actions cannot directly disable weapons, armour, or other essential combat gear (for example a priest's holy symbol).
    
- **Stapling.** Using a weapon to staple a target's clothes to a wall, door, or piece of furniture. The classic weapon options for this would be a thrown dagger or an arrow/quarrel, but any sharp weapon might be suitable. Stapling is only possible if the weapon can pass through loose clothing and embed in the surface behind the target; rigid or reinforced armour prevents this, though a cloak or tabard worn over such armour may be targeted. A character that is stapled suffers a 2 point penalty to AC and attacks and may not use movement. A character may free themselves at the end of one full round of uninterrupted effort during which they take no other actions and are not forced to defend themselves. If the character is interrupted or forced to take other actions (for example, being targeted by an attack), freeing themselves instead requires three full rounds of effort.

### Cover

When an enemy is unable to act (for example, stunned, dazed, pinned, unconscious, or surprised) an attacker may choose to be _prepared_ to immediately complete an attack in response to what the enemy does once they are again able to act. An unconscious character could wake up to find a knife pressed to their throat or an archer, bow drawn back, aiming at them and demanding their surrender.

A target may be covered by bows and crossbows within the short range of the weapon, or by a one-handed blade from an adjacent attacker. The attacker must be proficient with the weapon used for covering the target.

When a character declares that they are covering a target, they roll initiative as normal, but may make their first attack on the initiative count of the covered target if it precedes their own initiative by 4 or less (8 or less if the attacker has weapon specialization). This attack preempts the action taken by the target (an exception to actions on same initiative count being simultaneous). If the attacker's initiative precedes the target's, they may choose to complete the attack on their initiative, hold and continue covering the target waiting to see what they will do, or shift their attack to another target in range (losing the benefits of covering.)

In addition to preempting the target, the first attack roll is made with a +2 bonus, and the critical hit range is increased by 2 (barring other effects, natural rolls of 16 or better).

### Guard

When a combatant takes the guard action, they are prepared to immediately make a melee attack when an enemy approaches within reach or attacks them. A guarding character is considered to be _set for a charge_ gaining the -2 bonus to initiative, and with specific weapons (as indicated in the notes to the master weapons list), double-damage against a charging enemy.

When a character declares the guard action they still roll initiative as normal (with the -2 bonus for being set for a charge as already stated), however regardless of initiative score they may attack on the initiative count of a character that attacks them. If the attacker has _longer reach_ than the guarding character, the two attacks occur simultaneously, otherwise the guard's attack preempts the attacker's. The exception to this is a charging attacker; In this case the character with the longer reach weapon preempts their opponent, if the reach is equal the character with weapon of greater size acts first, and if both weapons are of equal size, the character with the lower initiative score acts first.  

At the end of the round, a guarding character that has not been attacked may abort the guard and use up to 1/2 their movement to make a single melee attack. If multiple characters have 'end of round' actions, they are resolved in order of initiative as normal.

### Parry

Sometimes the best option is to simply focus your effort on not getting hit and hope that by weathering the storm you will create an opening that your allies can exploit.

When a character chooses to parry they forfeit all other actions for the round, including movement and opportunity attacks, and focus solely on avoiding and deflecting melee attacks. This grants a bonus to armour class equal to one-half of their non-warrior levels (rounded up), or one-half of their warrior levels (rounded up) plus one against melee attacks. For dual- and multi-class character calculate the parry bonus for warrior and non-warrior levels separately then add them together. For example, a 5th level thief would gain a -3 bonus to armour class, a 5th level ranger would gain a -4 bonus, and a fighter 4/cleric 4 would gain a -5 bonus. If the parrying character has a shield it's normal AC bonus applies.

Parrying offers no benefit against missile attacks and only provides a benefit against spells where the caster must touch the target.

### Special Manoeuvres

Combatants do not just stand before each other trading strikes until one falls, they employ a variety of tools, tactics, and techniques to try and gain an edge over their opponent. Some of these are unique abilities of a class or kit - such as a thief's backstab - and detailed elsewhere while the list below are available to all characters who are appropriately equipped and meet any situational requirements.

- Block
    
- Disarm
    
- Grab Item
    
- Pull/Trip
    
- Sap
    
- Shield-Punch
    
- Shield-Rush
    
- Special Weapon Manoeuvres
    
- Trap (and Break)    

Some special manoeuvres are allowed to be declared on a character's turn, before they make an attack roll, rather than during initiative, but the timing of the declaration may affect how the manoeuvre functions. The DM may also choose to use these rules to adjudicate some situational (non-standard) actions, for example if a character rolls a cask down the stairs at enemies, a trip attack against each person in it's path might be appropriate.

For characters with more than one attack in a round, the special manoeuvres below use a single weapon attack (incurring the normal penalties for attacking with multiple weapons, if applicable). Additional attacks may be used as normal, if circumstances allow - A character could not, for example, make an additional attack with the weapon they are using to trap an enemy's blade.

#### Block

Blocking uses a single weapon attack to deflect an opponent's melee or thrown weapon strike. It can be an excellent tactic for character's with multiple attacks to use against opponents that make only one attack - they expend one attack to fend off the enemy, and attack with the remainder. Blocks can be attempted with shields and any weapon except rope-like weapons (whips, slings, etc.) however a mundane missile weapon (bow, crossbow, etc) used in this way is ruined unless the equipment's description states otherwise.

When a character attempts a block they roll a normal attack roll against AC4 and the attacking character makes a normal attack roll against the blocking character's AC. If the attacker fails the roll, the attack misses as normal, if the blocking character fails the roll and the attacker succeeds, the attack hits as normal. If both characters succeed on their rolls, the character with the _lower dice roll_ succeeds.

_For example, a fighter with an effective THAC0 of 14 is fighting a giant with an effective THAC0 of 9. The fighter attempts to block the giant's next attack. The giant makes a successful attack roll of 18, easily beating the fighter's AC 1. The fighter rolls an 11, which narrowly succeeds against AC4. Because 11 is the lower dice roll, the fighter is able to block the giant's attack, and avoid what would otherwise have been a critical hit._

A block can be declared before initiative is rolled, or on the character's turn, before they make an attack roll, but an attack can only be blocked after the block is declared (i.e. if declared on the character's turn it can only be used on attacks that occur _after_ their initiative count). The player does not have to use their block against the first attack directed at them - if they anticipate being attacked multiple times in a round it is their choice which to block - but if a block is not used by the end of the round it is wasted - it cannot be changed to a different action, and cannot be applied to an attack that has already been resolved.

With most weapons blocks can only be used against attacks directed at the blocking character, however when wielding a polearm, a block may be used to deflect attacks from large-size melee weapons against someone else, as long as _either_ the attacker _or_ the person being attacked is within the reach of the blocking character.

#### Disarm

Disarming is an attempt to knock a weapon or object (such as a wand, potion, or rod) out of an opponent's hand, and is most effective against opponents with poor attack bonuses. Disarms can be performed offensively or defensively - both are resolved using the same mechanics; they differ in when they occur, what weapons are allowed, and how they interact with initiative.

When a character attempts a disarm they roll a normal attack against AC 0 while the intended victim rolls against AC 4. A character using a two-handed weapon in either role gains a +4 bonus to the AC they must roll against. The disarm is successful if the character performing the disarm succeeds and their opponent fails, or - if both succeed their rolls - they have the lower dice roll. If the disarm is successful, the victim's weapon is knocked 1d10 feet away (or 1d3-1 squares if using a 5' grid) in a random direction. If the character successfully performing a disarm has attacks remaining in the round they may choose to immediately replace one with the cover action against the disarmed character. If the disarm was performed with a melee weapon, the usual requirement that covering be performed with a one-handed blade is ignored.

**Offensive disarms** may be attempted with melee or missile weapons (including thrown weapons) within short range. They must be declared before initiative is rolled, increase initiative by +1, and are resolved on the initiative count of the character attempting the disarm.

**Defensive disarms** may only be attempted with melee weapons but may be declared either before initiative or on the character's turn, before an attack roll. As with blocks, a defensive disarm can only be used against an attack that occurs after the disarm is declared, does not have to be used against the first attack directed at them, and are wasted if not used by the end of the round. If a defensive disarm is unsuccessful, the attack is then resolved normally.

It is impossible to disarm a weapon two sizes larger than the one used in the disarm attempt; a dagger cannot knock a staff from a wizard's hands. If a missile weapon is used in an attempt to offensively disarm, it is the size of missile itself, not the weapon that fires it, that is used in this determination. For objects without explicit size categories, the DM adjudicates using this as a guideline.

Attempting to **disarm a shield** is a special case - a successful attempt to disarm a shield does not tear it from the wielder's arm, but will knock it out of position, such that it no longer protects the wielder. For the remainder of the round the shield-wielder loses the AC bonus of the shield (including any magical bonus to AC). Any other special properties, say the shield radiates a fear effect, are not affected.

#### Grab

Grabbing is an attempt to take a weapon or object from an opponent (distinct from grabbing an opponent directly which is a grapple; refer to the rules of non-lethal attacks for more detail). To perform a grab a character must have at least one hand free (two is better of course), be within arm's reach, and must declare the attempt before making an attack roll. Obviously, attempting to grab something from a character that threatens you is dangerous, and the defender may immediately make an opportunity attack.

Grabbing begins similar to disarming, with the character attempting the grab rolling a normal attack against AC 0 and the intended victim rolling against AC 4 to see if they are able to get a grasp on the object. The DM may assign penalties to the grab attempt to reflect the circumstances and difficulty, for example trying to grab a tiny ring off someone's finger might incur a -8 penalty. In the event of a tie, and at the DM's discretion, the item being grabbed may instead be dropped or broken.

If the grabbing character successfully gets a grasp an opposed strength roll is then used to determine if they can wrench the object away from their opponent and take control of it. Each character rolls a d20 and must roll their strength score or _less_. Characters with exceptional strength (i.e. 18/xx) just use 18 for this check. If both succeed the character with the higher dice roll wins. If either character only uses one hand their strength score is reduced by 3 for the purpose of this check.

#### Pull/Trip

Tripping or pulling the legs out from under an opponent can be particularly effective when the target is moving at speed and especially when the target does not see it coming - a stationary target on the other hand is very difficult to knock down. A pull or trip can also be very effective against mounted enemies, either tripping the mount or pulling the rider off. This manoeuvre can be attempted with any long staff-like weapon - polearms, spears and harpoons, the footman's flail, mace or pick, bills and scythes, etc.... even bows and crossbows (except the hand variety) may be used. Chain and rope-like weapons including slings and bolas are also excellent options.

To pull or trip an enemy, the intention must be declared before an attack roll is made. The character then makes a normal attack roll. If successful an opposed ability roll is made pitting the attacker's strength against the defender's dexterity. For the purpose of this check the normal movement rate of a creature is considered it's dexterity if it does not have a dexterity score.

The attacker's strength is modified for the check as follows:  

-6 if the target is stationary  

-2 if target has 4 or more legs  

+3 if the target is unaware of the attack  

+6 if the target is moving faster than it's normal movement speed

Small creatures seem to have superior tripping technique - perhaps due to their lower centre of gravity - though the sheer mass of the most enormous creatures is more than enough to overcome their advantage. These factors affect the defender's dexterity check as follows:  

-2 if the attacker is small size or smaller  

+4 if the defender is huge size  

+6 if the defender is gargantuan size

If the attacker wins the opposed roll (being the only one to succeed, or succeeding with the higher dice roll), the target is knocked down. If the target was mounted, they additionally suffer 1d3 damage, and if the target was a mount the rider must succeed on a riding proficiency check to land on their feet or suffer this damage). If the attack was made with a rope-like weapon such as a lasso or lariat, the target was moving faster than it's normal speed, and the attacker loses the opposed roll the attacker must choose whether they release the weapon and are disarmed or maintain their grip and are knocked down and dragged.

#### Sap

Sapping is hitting an opponent's head with the aim of knocking them unconscious rather than killing. While a sap, or blackjack, is a made-for-purpose tool for doing this manoeuvre, it may be attempted with any melee weapon, striking with the flat of a blade, the haft of an axe, the pommel of a weapon, etc. Only medium-size creatures or smaller may be sapped - the mass (and also often thicker skulls) of larger enemies prevents the localized concussive trauma necessary to knock these creatures out.

A sapping attempt must be declared before initiative is rolled and increases initiative by 1. The sap is resolved with an attack roll against the target's unarmoured armour class (i.e. only natural armour, dexterity and magical bonuses apply) with either a -4 penalty, or a -8 penalty if the target is wearing any kind of helmet. If the attack is successful damage is rolled as normal for the weapon, _except that_ a weapon's magical bonuses to attack and damage do not apply unless the weapon is specifically intended for this use (such as a blackjack/sap) and the damage type is bludgeoning. Only 1/4 of the damage is 'real', the remainder is temporary. Ordinarily, for each point of total damage (real or temporary) inflicted there is a 5% chance (maximum 40%) that the target is knocked out. If however the victim is surprised, sleeping, restrained, or magically held, the chance of a knockout is instead 10% per point of damage (max 80%).

A sapped character remains unconscious for 2d8 full rounds.

**Mugging** is a special variant of sapping available only to thieves. Where a normal sap attempt uses brute force, mugging is a precision attack against a vulnerable point; consequently it can be used against creatures of up to large-size as long as the thief is able to reach the target's head. When making a mugging attack the thief may elect to have the attack do no damage at all (i.e. no chance of accidentally killing a target that was intended to be subdued) or to inflict entirely 'real' damage; mugging never inflicts temporary damage, and does not use the knockout percentage rules of sapping. In order to perform a mugging attempt the thief must meet the same conditions as performing a backstab attack (e.g. must be to the enemy's rear and the enemy must not be aware of the thief). The mugging attempt incurs a -4 penalty to the attack roll if the target is wearing a helmet that _fully encloses_ their head such as a great helm, but no penalty otherwise. If the attack is successful the target must make a saving throw versus petrification, modified by half the difference in level or hit dice between the mugger and the victim (rounded down; maximum +/- 6) . On a failure the creature is knocked out for 3d10 rounds. If the victim becomes aware of the thief before the mugging attack is resolved, the attempt automatically becomes a normal sapping attack instead, subject to all the usual rules and restrictions for sapping.

Creatures that do not have a discernible head-like anatomy or who are immune to bludgeoning damage cannot be sapped nor mugged.

#### Shield-Punch

Any character with a shield equipped may sacrifice it's protection (losing it's AC bonus for the round) and instead use it to strike an opponent. The character may choose either to substitute the shield punch for their normal attack, or to use it as a secondary weapon following the usual rules for fighting with multiple weapons (ordinarily -2 to attack with the primary weapon, and -4 with the secondary, slower weapon's speed is used for initiative). The intent to shield punch must be declared before initiative is rolled.

Shield punches can be useful if a character finds themselves disarmed, of if they wish to exploit an opportunity to make some extra attacks against an enemy while remaining ready to switch back to a more defensive posture. The alternative - dropping or stowing the shield, drawing a new weapon to fight with, then retrieving or re-equipping the the shield afterwards - consumes much more effort.

#### Shield-Rush

A shield-rush is an attempt to knock someone down by ramming into them shield-first. It is a risky manoeuvre as the attacker can easily end up knocked down themselves whether or not they bring down the enemy.

In order to perform a shield rush a character must have a minimum of 2 unoccupied squares (10') between themselves and the intended target. The manoeuvre replaces one attack, and is declared on the character's turn, before making an attack roll. While it is not a proper charge (i.e. does not gain the benefits of a charge), it is still treated as one in respect of the damage dealt by defenders who are set to receive a charge. In addition the attacker loses the defensive benefits of the shield for the rest of the round.

The shield-rush is resolved with a normal attack roll, if it hits, an opposed strength roll is made - if both characters fail they both fall down, otherwise only the loser of the opposed roll is knocked down.

The attacker's strength is modified as follows:  

4 point bonus or penalty for each size category of difference between the attacker and defender.  

+3 if the defender was not aware of the shield-rush  

-2 if the defender has 4 legs or more.

#### Special Weapon Manoeuvres

Some weapon descriptions describe special manoeuvres that can be performed with a specific weapon (and in some cases the special weapon manoeuvre is the _only_ way to use the weapon). These weapons include bolas, chain, harpoon, lasso, mancatcher, and net. Using these special features is treated similar to a called shot, incurring a +1 penalty to initiative, and a -4 to attack. Unlike a called shot that requires precise aim, using these attacks requires precise technique, consequently they are not disrupted in the same manner as called shots.

#### Trap/Pin

Traps and Pins are defensive manoeuvres that deny an opponent the use of their weapon ; the difference between the two is mostly semantic - a trap catches an attacker's weapon and prevents the wielder from pulling it back towards themselves, while a pin holds a weapon in place - often pushed back against the wielder or a nearby surface. It can be a very effective tactic for a dual wielder to use against a single-weapon enemy, or for allies to use in concert - one character pinning an enemy's weapon while another makes attacks from a flank or rear. The person performing a pin or trap also loses use of the weapon (or shield) being used while the hold continues.

Traps are resolved when a character is attacked, and before the attack is resolved; the defender attempting the trap makes a normal attack against AC 0 while the attacker whose weapon they are attempting to trap rolls against AC 4. If the defender wins the opposed roll the weapon is trapped or pinned - otherwise the attacker may continue to make their attack roll against the defender as normal.

If the attacker's weapon is successfully trapped or pinned they lose any additional strikes _with that weapon_ in the current round. They may still however - if they have additional strikes in the current round - attack with a secondary weapon (suffering the normal penalties of dual wielding) or abandon the weapon and execute attacks with another equipped weapon without the penalties of dual wielding. If the character chooses to keep hold of their weapon they may make an opposed strength roll at the end of the round to try and free their weapon.

In subsequent rounds, if the trapped/pinned weapon has not been abandoned, the trapping/pinning character may declare at initiative that they are maintaining the hold. Opposed strength checks are made on the earlier inititative count of the two characters, and again at the end of each round, until the weapon is either freed or abandoned. While a character continues to keep hold of a trapped/pinned weapon they may not make opportunity attacks.

## Non-Lethal Attack

Sometimes the aim of attacking is to subdue an opponent without killing. Corpses are difficult to interrogate, civil society frowns on killing an accused before trial, and many find it distasteful to kill a comrade who is compelled by magic to attack them. There are a variety of non-lethal options that may be available to characters including pummelling, wrestling, and martial arts. Characters may also attempt to use certain weapons in a non-lethal manner (for example striking with the flat of a sword) or simply overbear their enemy. Non-Lethal Attacks are also often a fall-back for characters who might find themselves caught unarmed or who are disarmed during a fight.

By default the damage inflicted by attacks that are non-lethal is only 25% (rounded down) real damage - the residual amount is temporary damage. There are specific exceptions given, and the DM may make others case-by-case, but in such cases should inform players if the balance of real vs. temporary damage will be different before they commit to the attack. While temporary damage automatically heals at a rate of 1 point per round, if the total amount of temporary damage is great enough it can still lead to a creature's death.

In choosing to perform a non-lethal attack, remember that all _unarmed_ attacks while threatened by an enemy trigger an attack of opportunity.

### Pummelling

Pummelling includes most attacks made with hands, fists, elbows, and similar appendages. Any creature of average intelligence that possesses appropriate limbs can attempt pummelling attacks, though creatures with natural weapons typically use those instead.

To make a pummelling attack requires **at least one hand free**, but the hand does **not** need to be bare or empty. Wearing a gauntlet, using the pommel of a weapon, or improvising a weapon with whatever is at hand (bottles, mugs, etc.) are all allowed.

Pummelling can **only** be attempted against targets that are:

- Alive
    
- Organic
    
- Non-vegetable
    
- Non-fluid

Undead, golems, and jellies, for example, cannot be pummelled. With limited exceptions, pummelling is ineffective against creatures who can only be harmed by special or magical weapons and is **always** ineffective against creatures immune to bludgeoning damage.

Finally, as a general rule, pummelling requires the ability to strike at the target’s head or torso. Consequently, it usually **cannot** be used against a creature more than **one size larger** than the attacker. The DM may make exceptions if the attacker has a way to reach the target’s head height (flying, a platform, or a prone target).

#### Attacks Per Round

Without specialization, most characters may make a **single pummelling attack per round**. If both hands are free, they may make two attacks, suffering the usual penalties for attacking with two weapons.

A pummelling attack is resolved using a normal attack roll. **Exception:** a roll of 1 is **not** an automatic miss.

- If fighting with **bare fists**, the modified attack roll determines the type of punch and the damage inflicted (see the table below).
    
- If attacking with **objects**, damage depends on the size and nature of the object:
 
|**Object Type**|**Damage**|
|---|---|
|Small, soft|1d2|
|Small, hard|1d3|
|Large, soft|1d4|
|Large, hard|1d6|

**Limits on improvised weapons:**

- Cannot exceed **1/3 of the attacker's maximum press**
    
- Greatest dimension cannot exceed **half the attacker's height**

Some gear (e.g., the cestus) is a special case — refer to its description for details.

#### Strength & Pulling Punches

High strength grants a bonus to damage, but a character may **choose to pull their punch**, negating strength damage or even all damage. The decision must be declared **after the damage roll** but **before damage is applied**.

Even if no damage is inflicted, a successful pummelling attack has a **chance to knock out** the opponent, as indicated in the **Punch Table**. The attacker rolls percentile dice; if the result is below the table value, the target is stunned for **1d10 rounds**.

#### Knockdown

On a successful hit that **does not result in a knockout**, the attacker may attempt to knock the target down. Make an **opposed Strength check** with the following modifiers:

- +2 per size category difference (bonus if attacker larger, penalty if smaller)
    
- +1 if using a large object
    
- Specialization, mastery, or grand mastery bonuses in pummelling apply    

The defender is knocked down if the attacker **wins the opposed strength roll**. To succeed, the attacker must pass their check **and** have a higher roll than the defender (if the defender also succeeds).

#### Critical Hits

Critical hits in pummelling have a special effect:

- The defender rolls **Save vs. Death**, applying the attacker’s hit probability adjustment as a bonus or penalty
    
- Failure results in **3d10 rounds of unconsciousness**

### Wrestling

**Wrestling** is an attempt to seize physical control of an opponent: first by gaining a **hold**, and then by maintaining that hold or developing it into a **lock** in order to force submission or render the opponent unconscious.

Any creature of at least semi-intelligent species (**Int 3+**) with appropriate appendages for grasping and restraining an enemy may attempt wrestling attacks.

- Limbless creatures such as worms generally cannot wrestle, though exceptions exist (such as constrictor snakes, which are assumed to employ a form of wrestling).
    
- Worm- and snake-like creatures **resist all damage** from wrestling attacks, but holds and locks still prevent them from making attacks until they break free.
    
- Creatures immune to normal weapons resist damage from wrestling attacks (unless the attacker functions as a magical weapon), but may still be grappled or pinned and still suffer damage from locks.
    
- Incorporeal or amorphous creatures can neither make nor be the victim of wrestling attacks.
    
#### Requirements and Restrictions

Wrestling is strictly **one-on-one**, involving a single attacker and a single defender, though other characters may later attempt to assist one of the wrestlers.

To wrestle, the attacker must have **both hands free**. Even carrying a shield prevents wrestling, as it interferes with maintaining a secure grip. armour also restricts movement and leverage, imposing penalties based on its type:
 
|Type of armour|Wrestling Modifier|
|---|---|
|Studded Leather|–1|
|Chain, Ring, and Scale Mail|–2|
|Banded, Splint, and Plate Mail|–5|
|Field Plate armour|–8|
|Full Plate armour|–10|

Characters without proficiency in wrestling may attempt **only one** wrestling attack per round.

- **Non-warriors** with wrestling proficiency may make as many attempts to secure or improve a hold as their normal attacks per round allow, but once they make a successful wrestling attack, they may take no further actions that round.
    
- **Warriors** with wrestling proficiency may use their full allotment of attacks. After the first successful wrestling attempt, any subsequent successes may be used to improve a hold or switch locks.

#### Initiating a Wrestling Attack

At the start of a wrestling attempt, one of two situations applies:

**1. Both combatants declared wrestling attacks** for the round (common in sporting or formal wrestling).  

In this case, no initial attack roll is required. The attacker moves into the defender’s square and proceeds directly to determining whether a hold is established.

**2. The defender declared any other action** (typical in combat).  

In this case, the attacker must first make a wrestling attack roll.

#### Wrestling Attack Roll

When required, the attacker makes an attack roll against the defender’s **natural (unarmoured) armour Class** (typically AC 10 for humanoids), modified by Dexterity and magical bonuses.

armour protects well against slashing, crushing, and piercing blows, but does little to prevent limbs from being twisted, pulled, or restrained. Magical effects that provide a _flat armour Class_ rather than a bonus (such as shield spells or bracers of defence) count as **+1 to AC**, regardless of enchantment strength.

- On a **successful attack**, the attacker moves into the defender’s square, immediately inflicts **1 point of damage** (unless immune), and proceeds to determine whether a hold is established.
    
- On a **critical hit**, a hold is automatically established, **3 points of damage** are inflicted (unless immune), and the attacker may immediately attempt to improve the hold to a lock.

#### Establishing or Improving a Hold

To establish a hold—or to improve a hold into a lock—the combatants make an **opposed attack roll** against each other’s natural armour Class (with Dexterity and magical modifiers as above).

Strength bonuses apply, and the attacker also gains the following modifiers:

- **±2** per size category difference between attacker and defender (bonus if larger)
    
- **–1** if the defender is immune to normal weapons
    
- **–2** if the defender has an unusually supple body (snakes, eels, worms, etc.)

If a hold was not automatically established, the attacker must **win the opposed roll** (i.e. achieve the lower successful roll).

- If the attacker does **not** win, they are pushed back into their original square. Both combatants retain their original facing.
    
- If the attacker **wins**, the defender suffers **1d2 damage + the attacker’s Strength bonus** (unless immune) and becomes **grappled**.

A grappled creature cannot move, including changing facing, until they break free or are released. Creatures that are grappling or grappled **may not make opportunity attacks**.

#### Options While Grappling

When a character has a hold or lock and is eligible to make another attack, they may choose to:

- **Release** the opponent
    
- **Maintain** the existing hold or lock
    
- **Improve** the hold to a lock, or switch to a different lock

If the grappler releases their opponent, they may:

1. Immediately make another unarmed attack against a target within the distance they can move, or
    
2. Draw a weapon and, on an initiative count **2 later**, move up to half their movement and make an attack, or
    
3. Attempt any other action normally available to them that is not casting a spell.

#### Maintaining a Hold or Lock

To maintain an existing hold or lock, the combatants make another **opposed attack roll**.

- If the **grappled character wins**, their situation improves:
    
    - A lock becomes a hold
        
    - A hold is broken entirely
        
- If the **grappler wins**, the hold or lock is maintained and the grappled character suffers damage:
    
    - **1d2 damage** for a hold
        
    - Lock damage as described in the lock entry
        
- If the result is a **tie**, or if both characters fail their rolls, the hold or lock is maintained but no damage is dealt.

#### Improving or Switching Locks

An opposed attack roll is also made when a grappler attempts to improve a hold into a lock, or to switch from one lock to another.

- If the **grappled character wins**, they inflict **1d2 damage** on the grappler and their situation improves by one step.
    
- If the grappled character wins with a **critical hit**, they completely reverse the situation, becoming the grappler and immediately choosing which lock their opponent is subjected to.
    
- If the **grappler wins**, they successfully improve the hold to a lock or select a different lock.
    
- If the result is a tie or both fail, there is no change.

#### Actions While Grappled

A character that begins their initiative **grappled** may only:

- Make a wrestling attack
    
- Make a pummelling attack
    
- Make a melee attack with a **size S weapon** or a natural weapon, if their facing allows

All attacks must be directed at the creature grappling them.

Pummelling attacks and melee attacks with size S weapons suffer a **–2 penalty to hit**.

- If a grappled character scores a **critical hit** with a weapon attack, they may choose either the normal critical effect **or** to immediately break the grapple.
    
- If a non-wrestling attack results in a **knock-out or knock-down**, the grapple is immediately broken.

If the grappled character makes a wrestling attack, another opposed roll is made:

- If neither character succeeds, or if the grappler wins, there is no change—unless the grappler scores a critical hit, in which case a hold becomes a lock (or an existing lock may be switched).
    
- If the grappled character wins, they inflict **1d2 damage** and their situation normally improves by one step.
    
- If the grappled character wins with a **critical hit**, they immediately reverse the situation and gain a lock on their opponent (the 1d2 damage still applies).

#### Wrestling Locks

When a character is placed in a **lock**, they suffer one of the following effects, chosen by the character that gained the lock. Unless otherwise noted, the grappler’s Strength bonuses apply to all damage inflicted.

##### Takedown

The grappler forces the locked character violently to the ground.

- The locked character becomes **prone** and suffers **1d3 damage**
    
- The character remains **locked**
    
- Each subsequent round the takedown is maintained, the locked character suffers **1 additional point of damage per round**, including the current one _(1st round: 1d3; 2nd round: 2; 3rd round: 3; etc.)_

##### Throw

The grappler hurls the locked character away.

- The locked character is thrown to any square of the grappler’s choice within **10 feet**
    
- The thrown character suffers **1d4 damage** (**1d4 + 1** if thrown onto a particularly hard or unyielding surface such as stone)
    
- The thrown character lands **prone** and is **no longer grappled**

Additional effects:

- If the thrown character strikes another creature, that creature is immediately subjected to an **overbearing attack**, using the throwing character’s Strength and the thrown creature’s size
    
- A character thrown into a hazard (spikes, burning oil, etc.) suffers the hazard’s damage in addition to the damage from the throw

Restrictions:

- A creature **two or more size categories larger** than the grappler cannot be thrown; such an attempt is treated as a **takedown** instead
    
- If the thrown creature is the same size as the grappler or smaller, the grappler may move up to **half their movement rate** before making the throw

##### Slam

The grappler smashes the locked character into the ground or another nearby surface.

- The locked character is hurled to any square of the grappler’s choice within **5 feet**, including the current square
    
- The character suffers **1d8 damage** (**1d8 + 1** if the surface is particularly hard)
    
- The locked character becomes **prone**
    
- If applicable, the character also suffers damage from any hazard in the destination square

The grappler moves into the chosen square but does **not** suffer hazard damage; the grappled creature absorbs the impact.

If the grappled character successfully makes a **saving throw vs breath weapons**, their situation improves and they become only **held**.

##### Press

The grappler applies crushing pressure or painful joint manipulation.

- The locked character suffers **1d6 + 1 damage**
    
- For each subsequent round the press is maintained, the damage bonus increases by **+1** _(e.g. the 4th round would deal 1d6 + 4 damage)_

##### Hammer

The grappler repeatedly pounds the grappled character against a surface or delivers brutal blows.

- The grappled character suffers **1d2 damage**
    
- The grappled character must make a **saving throw vs death**
    
- On a failure, they are **knocked unconscious for 3d10 rounds**

The normal rules for **pummelling** do **not** apply to this attack.

##### Manipulate

The grappler gains limited control over the grappled character’s body.

- The grappled character suffers **1d2 damage** from being roughly handled
    
- The grappler may:
    
    - Pry away a held object
        
    - Remove exposed equipment
        
    - Bind the character
        
    - Negate a natural attack
        
    - Inhibit the character’s actions in other ways, at the DM’s discretion

Time required:

- **One attack action**: pry away a held object, remove a piece of equipment, or affix a manacle to a single limb
    
- **Two attack actions**: tie a rope to a single limb

If the DM determines a piece of equipment is especially difficult to remove (such as a helmet or tightly fitted armour), the grappler must succeed on:

- An **Open Doors** roll if the opponent is of equal size or smaller, or
    
- A **Bend Bars** roll if the opponent is larger

##### Carry

The grappler lifts the grappled character and transports them.

- The grappled creature may be carried up to the grappler’s **normal movement rate**
    
- The grappler cannot carry a creature whose total weight (including equipment) exceeds their **maximum press**

#### Assistance in Wrestling

Although wrestling itself is strictly one-on-one, other characters may attempt to **assist** a wrestler.

##### Attacking a Wrestler

Other characters may make **melee**, **pummelling**, or **martial arts** attacks against either wrestler.

- A **critical hit** or **knock-down** against the **grappler** immediately breaks the hold or lock
    
- Missile- and grenade-like attacks are also possible, but:
    
    - They carry a significant risk of striking an ally
        
    - They **do not** break a grapple, even on a successful hit

#### Overbearing a Wrestler

Allies may attempt to **overbear** a wrestler.

- The grappler and the grappled creature **do not count** toward the overbearing attempt
    
- If the overbearing attempt is successful, the grappler’s available lock choices are limited to:
    
    - **Takedown**
        
    - **Press**
        
    - **Hammer**
        
    - **Manipulate** (at the DM’s discretion)

This limitation applies regardless of whether the overbearing attempt was made by allies or enemies of the grappler.

#### Wrestling a Wrestler

A wrestling attack may be made **only against the grappler**, never against the grappled creature.

- If the wrestling attack is successful:
    
    - The grappler immediately loses their hold or lock
        
    - The previously grappled creature is freed
        
    - The grappler is pulled into the attacker’s square and becomes the **grappled** creature _(facing does not change)_

#### Opportunity Attacks

Making a wrestling attack—including maintaining a hold or lock—**triggers opportunity attacks** from any threatening creatures that are **not unarmed humanoids**, as detailed in the opportunity attack rules.

### Overbearing

**Overbearing** is an attempt to rush and pile onto an enemy _en masse_, often one who would cut individual attackers to pieces.

Whether the characters performing an overbearing attempt have weapons in hand or not, **overbearing is considered an unarmed attack**. This includes the initial attempt as well as any skill checks made to pin or maintain a pin, and therefore **triggers opportunity attacks** as detailed in that section.

The attempt to overbear resolves on the **initiative count of the slowest (last) attacker** participating in the attempt.

#### Making an Overbearing Attempt

A single attack roll is made using the **best THAC0** among the attackers, with a **+1 bonus to hit for each additional attacker** involved. This attack is made against the target’s **natural (unarmoured) Armour Class**, modified only by Dexterity and magical adjustments.

As with wrestling, magical effects that provide a _flat Armour Class_ rather than a bonus (such as shield spells or bracers of defence) count as **+1 to AC**, regardless of the strength of the enchantment.

If the attack roll is successful, an **opposed Strength check** is made to determine whether the attackers are able to drag the target down. This check uses the Strength of the **strongest attacker**, with the following modifiers:

- **±2** per size category difference between the largest attacker and the defender (bonus if larger)
    
- **+1** for each additional attacker
    
- **–4** if the defender has more than two legs or naturally has no legs (serpents, worms, etc.)
    
- **–4** if the defender is amorphous (slimes, oozes, jellies, etc.) _(as these creatures most often also lack legs, the total penalty is typically –8)_

#### Results of the Strength Check

If the attackers **do not win** the opposed Strength check, the defender shrugs off the attempt and holds their position. All attackers remain in, or return to, the squares they occupied before the attempt.

If the attackers **do win**, all participating attackers move into the defender’s space (up to the maximum number that can fit within the defender’s square or squares). Any attackers that do not fit occupy adjacent squares but still participate in the overbearing attempt.

The defender becomes **prone** and is unable to take any other action for the remainder of the round, including movement or opportunity attacks. The attackers are treated as though they are **kneeling**, and are likewise unable to take any other action for the remainder of the round.

Before initiative is rolled for the following round, the attackers must declare whether they will attempt to **pin** the overborne creature or **release** them and take a different action (which may not be casting a spell).

#### Releasing the Overborne Creature

An attacker that releases their hold suffers the normal penalties for rising from kneeling to standing (**+2 to initiative, –2 to subsequent attacks**) and acts on their initiative count as normal.

If **all** attackers release their hold, then on the initiative count of the slowest (last) attacker involved in the overbearing attempt, the released creature may immediately:

- Jump to their feet (an exception to the normal rules for rising from prone),
    
- Move up to half their movement rate, and
    
- Make an unarmed attack, an attack with the weapon they were wielding before being overborne, or perform another action normally available to them that is not casting a spell.

#### Attempting to Pin

If any attackers attempt to pin the overborne creature, another opposed Strength check is made on the **initiative count of the fastest (first) attacker** involved. This check uses the **highest Strength** among the attackers that are still participating, and applies the same modifiers as above.

- If the defender wins, all attackers must immediately leave the defender’s square, moving up to half their movement rate. These attackers do not suffer opportunity attacks from the creature that was overborne.
    
- If the attackers win, the overborne creature is **pinned**, as detailed below.
    
- If there is a tie, or if neither side succeeds on the Strength check, there is no change this round. Before the next initiative is rolled, the attackers may again choose to release the creature or re-attempt the pin.

#### Effect of Pins

Being pinned beneath a pile of enemies is an extremely dangerous position. However, so long as the overborne character can avoid being **fully immobilized**, there remains a slim chance they can force their way free through sheer effort.

Unless the character is simultaneously **overborne** _and_ **the victim of a wrestling hold or lock**, they may—subject to the limbs they have free at that moment—act on their initiative count despite being prone and grappled. Such actions are limited to:

- Unarmed attacks
    
- Natural weapon attacks
    
- Melee attacks with a **size S weapon** they were wielding when overborne

#### Facing and Threats

Facing does **not** apply to pins. All attackers involved in the pin both **threaten**, and are **threatened by**, the pinned creature.

#### Immobilizing Limbs

If any attackers have their hands free (remembering that weapons may be dropped as a free action), they may, on their individual initiative counts, choose to shift their effort toward **immobilizing the pinned character’s limbs**.

An immobilized limb **cannot be used** for as long as the pin is maintained.

- Immobilizing a limb of a target that is **more than one size category larger** than the attacker requires **one additional attacker with hands free** for each additional size category difference  
    _(e.g. two Medium attackers are required to immobilize each limb of a Huge creature)_
    
- Conversely, when the attackers are **larger than the target**, they may immobilize **one additional limb per size category difference** _(e.g. a single Huge creature could immobilize three limbs of a Medium creature)_

#### Maintaining the Pin

On the initiative count of the **last (slowest) attacker** involved in the pin, an **opposed Strength check** is made to determine whether the pin can be maintained. This check uses the same modifiers as the initial overbearing attempt, with the following exceptions:

- Any attacker currently immobilizing a limb is **not counted** as an additional attacker for this check
    
- A defender who has had **all limbs immobilized** makes this check with **half their Strength**

**Outcomes:**

- If the defender **wins the opposed Strength check**, or rolls a **natural 1**, they slip free and the pin is broken
    
- If the result is a **tie**, or if neither side succeeds on the Strength check, the pin is maintained, but **no additional immobilization may be attempted in the following round**

#### Fully Immobilized Defenders

If a defender begins a round with **all limbs immobilized**, they are unable to make any attacks. Their only chance at an unassisted escape is to either:

- Win the opposed Strength check made on the initiative count of the last (slowest) attacker maintaining the pin, or
    
- Roll a **natural 1** on that check

Beginning in this round, and for as long as the character remains pinned, **each time the attackers win an opposed Strength check** they may choose to apply the effects of **one** of the following wrestling locks:

- **Press**
    
- **Hammer**
    
- **Manipulate**

#### Assistance When Overborne

There is no assisting the attackers in an overbearing attempt—either a character is participating in the attempt or they are not.

The defender’s allies, however, may attempt to assist in different ways. Successful overbearing or wrestling attacks made against attackers participating in the overbearing attempt pull that attacker free of the pile and into the ally’s square. A weapon attack that results in a critical hit or knockdown causes the struck attacker to immediately release the defender.

Missile attacks fired into the pile have a chance to strike any character involved (a called shot cannot negate this), but as there are often many enemies and only one ally, this may still be a risk worth taking if the pile cannot be reached.

As overbearing is considered an unarmed attack—and as attackers may need to drop weapons to immobilize limbs—each armed ally that threatens the square may make opportunity attacks, up to the maximum number allowed in a round, against attackers involved in the overbearing attempt. Needless to say, an armed ally will often break up an overbearing attempt very quickly.

### Martial Arts

Martial arts represent disciplined systems of unarmed combat developed through long practice and tradition. Unlike other forms of non-lethal fighting, martial arts require **formal training** and are not universally available.

Access to martial arts proficiency may be **tightly restricted by the DM** to reflect the tone and culture of the campaign world. In some settings, martial arts may be known only to characters from specific regions, cultures, or organizations (such as monastic orders, religious sects, or assassin guilds), or may require locating a skilled practitioner willing to provide instruction. Players should consult the DM and the _Proficiencies_ chapter for details on acquiring martial arts training.

In addition, martial arts are not a single discipline. Characters learn **specific styles**, each with its own strengths, limitations, and techniques. Some styles emphasize mobility and defense, others integrate weapons, and some focus on disabling or controlling opponents rather than dealing damage.

#### Martial Arts Styles

Each martial arts style specifies:

- The **base damage** of unarmed attacks
    
- The **armour class bonus** granted by the style
    
- The primary **striking body part** (hands, feet, legs, or body)
    
- Which **special manoeuvres** may be learned
    
- Which **weapons (if any)** may be used in combination with the style

**Note:** Knowing a martial arts style does **not** grant proficiency with any weapon, nor does it grant training in using a weapon in conjunction with the style. Weapon proficiency and style-specific training must be learned separately, as described in the _Proficiencies_ chapter.

A character who knows more than one martial arts style may change styles from round to round, but **may not mix styles within the same round**. The style being used must be declared **before initiative is rolled**. If a character only knows one style, they only need to declare that they are making a martial arts attack.

### Attacks per Round

Regardless of style, a character using martial arts **while unarmed** gains **one additional attack per round**.

If a weapon is used in combination with a martial arts style, this additional attack is **not gained**.

### Opportunity Attacks and Martial Arts

Martial arts are unique among unarmed fighting techniques in that they have been developed specifically to counter armed opponents.

When a martial artist makes their **first unarmed attack while engaging a given armed opponent**, that attack **triggers opportunity attacks as normal**.

Once engaged, further unarmed martial arts attacks against the same opponent do **not** provoke additional opportunity attacks unless the martial artist:

- Voluntarily leaves the opponent’s threatened area
    
- Changes facing such that the opponent is now to their rear
    
- Makes an unarmed attack against a **different** threatening opponent

#### Martial Arts in Armour

Wearing armour does not prevent a character from making martial arts attacks, but the restricted movement makes them harder to execute effectively.

Characters using martial arts while wearing armour suffer the same penalties as wrestlers in armour:

|Type of Armour|Attack Modifier|
|---|---|
|Studded Leather|–1|
|Chain, Ring, and Scale Mail|–2|
|Banded, Splint, and Plate|–5|
|Field Plate|–8|
|Full Plate|–10|

#### Making a Martial Arts Attack

Unarmed martial arts attacks follow the same general limitations as pummelling attacks. They are generally ineffective against creatures that can only be harmed by special or magical weapons, as well as creatures immune to bludgeoning damage.

Valid targets must typically be:

- Alive
    
- Organic
    
- Non-vegetable
    
- Non-fluid

Martial arts attacks are resolved as **normal melee attacks** (attack roll vs. target AC), with the following exceptions:

- The penalties for attacking with two weapons do **not** apply if:
    
    - The primary hand makes an unarmed martial arts attack or an attack with a weapon the character has trained to use with the style, **and**
        
    - The off-hand makes an unarmed martial arts attack
        

#### Martial Arts Damage

Successful attacks made with weapons deal **real damage** as normal.

For **unarmed martial arts attacks**, damage is determined by:

- The **base damage** of the martial arts style
    
- The attacker’s **Strength modifier**
    
- A modifier based on the type of strike, determined by the **unmodified attack roll**

The descriptions below are narrative only and have no additional mechanical effect.

Martial arts attacks **do not score critical hits**.

Instead, on a **natural roll of 20**, the target must make a **saving throw vs. paralyzation**. If the save fails, compare the unmodified saving throw roll to the target’s Hit Dice or level:

- If the roll is **lower**, the target is **stunned** for 1d6 rounds
    
- If the roll is **equal or higher**, the target is **incapacitated** for 1d3 hours

#### Special Martial Arts Manoeuvres

Special manoeuvres represent advanced techniques learned as a martial artist increases skill in a particular style.

Manoeuvres fall into two broad categories:

- **Constant abilities**, which are always in effect
    
- **Active techniques**, which must be attempted during combat

Which manoeuvres are available, and how they are learned, are detailed in the _Proficiencies_ chapter. The mechanical effects of each manoeuvre are described individually below.

Each manoeuvre description specifies:

- Its effect
    
- Any requirements or limitations
    
- The consequences of a failed attack roll
    
- Which weapons (if any) may be used with the manoeuvre

To use a weapon with a manoeuvre, **all** of the following must apply:

1. The character is proficient with the weapon
    
2. The weapon is allowed by the martial arts style
    
3. The character has been trained to use the weapon with the style
    
4. The weapon is compatible with the manoeuvre

When a weapon is used, the attacker chooses whether to apply the weapon’s damage or the damage listed for the manoeuvre (typically choosing the higher value). All other effects apply as described.

### Special Martial Arts Manoeuvre Descriptions

#### Principal Method: Block

Blocks are purely defensive manoeuvres that help the martial artist avoid being struck by attacks. In a round where a character has declared a martial arts attack, these blocks may be declared either on the martial artist's initiative count or when the martial artist is hit by an attack, before damage is rolled, as long as they have sufficient attacks remaining in the round. Blocks are only effective against attacks from the direction of the character's front or flank, and only when the character is aware of the attack. Attack rolls made to resolve a block do not trigger opportunity attacks; however any offensive actions taken while blocking do.

##### Basic Parry:

The basic parry prevents a single melee attack from hitting the martial artist. It does not work against missile attacks.

The martial artist rolls an attack against his opponent, taking into account all normal adjustments to his opponent's AC as well as any penalty for martial arts manoeuvres against armour described in the previous section. If the attack hits, the martial artist has parried the triggering blow.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** The incoming attack hits.

**Weapons Allowed:** Any. Characters using weapons designed for parrying (sai, jitte, parrying dagger, etc) receive a +2 bonus to hit with the basic parry.

##### Parry All:

Parry all is a more advanced form of the basic parry allowing the martial artist a chance to deflect every melee attack in the round. It requires expending two of the character's attacks for the round. With this expenditure, the martial artist may roll as described in the basic parry against every eligible melee attack. She makes a separate attack roll against each oncoming blow.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** An opponent's blow hits.

**Weapons Allowed:** Any. Characters using weapons designed for parrying (sai, jitte, parrying dagger, etc) receive a +2 bonus to hit with the parry all manoeuvre.

##### Grappling Block:

With this advanced manoeuvre, the martial artist is able to get a hold of the weapon while parrying, making it harder for the attacker to attempt another attack. The grappling block requires both hands to perform - if unarmed the the martial artist might, for example, clap the blade of an opponent's sword between his palms, preventing it from striking and being swung again, where an armed martial artist uses a free hand to hold the opponent's weapon against their own.

The grappling block consumes one of the martial artist's attacks. If it is successful, the martial artist and the attacker both have a grip on the attacker's weapon, and the grip is never a dangerous one for the martial artist.

The attacker must get their weapon free before they may attempt another attack with it, and this requires the attacker to use an attack to make a strength check. If the attacker succeeds their weapon is free. If they fail the roll by 4 or more, the martial artist is able to wrench the weapon from their grasp and take full control over it. Any other result leaves the two combatants still contesting possession of the weapon. The attacker may make as many strength checks per round as he has attacks available, and can abandon the weapon voluntarily at the start of their initiative count before any rolls are made.

If hands are not the principal attack of the martial artists's style, while the weapon is still held by both the martial artist and their attacker the martial artist may make unarmed attacks on their opponent as normal, with a +2 bonus to the attack. Any other character making an attack on either combatant likewise enjoys a +2 bonus to hit.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** The attack hits, and if the martial artist attempted the grappling block with a weapon, it has been fouled on his opponent's weapon. The attacker immediately rolls a second attack roll (which does not consume an attack or strike) against the martial artist and if successful they manage to disarm the martial artist in the process of freeing their own weapon.

**Weapons Allowed:** Any. Characters using chain- or rope-like weapons receive a +2 bonus to hit with the grappling block manoeuvre.

##### Arrow Parry:

This manoeuvre is the pinnacle of blocks, allowing the martial artist to deflect thrown and missile weapons. It operates identical to Parry All, except that it applies to all eligible missile attacks, rather than melee attacks (i.e. costs two attacks, separate attack roll made against each incoming blow). A character that has also learned the Parry All manoeuvre may instead choose to expend 3 attacks to perform both, blocking all melee and missile attacks.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The weapon strikes the martial artist.

**Weapons Allowed:** Any. Characters using shields receive a +3 bonus to hit when parrying missile weapons as part of this manoeuvre.

#### Principal Method: Kick

##### Circle Kick

In this dramatic kick, the attacker builds up power and momentum by spinning in a complete circle before landing the kick. The manoeuvre can also be performed as a snap or thrust kick.

A successful kick (requiring a normal attack roll) adds 1d2 hp to the normal martial arts damage.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** As she tries to recover her balance, the martial artist loses the next attack she would ordinarily be allowed in this round, or the first attack of the following round, and her armour Class is worsened by 2 points until the next round.

**Weapons Allowed**: None.

##### Flying Kick:

This spectacular kick requires at least 5 feet of running space (i.e. there must be at least 1 empty square between the martial artist and their target and the martial artist must have sufficient movement). The character leaps high into the air and leads with a powerful kick to the head or upper body. If the kick connects, the character adds 1d4 hp to the normal martial arts damage.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The attacker falls to the ground adjacent to the intended target and prone and must spend one round getting back to his feet (unless he has an ability that allows him to recover faster).

**Weapons Allowed:** None.

##### Backward Kick:

This seemingly innocuous kick is extremely difficult to master. The character attempts a normal attack at any creature to their rear and within reach, either kicking his foot forward and straight up, with his toe ending up behind his own head to connect with the enemy, or lashing out straight backward. He does not have to turn around and face his attacker. The kick does normal damage.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The attacker suffers no ill effects.

**Weapons Allowed**: None.

#### Principal Method: Lock

Locking is the art of gripping an opponent in a way that prevents him from acting: twisting an arm so the joint is locked, applying pressure to choke off breathing and blood flow, etc.

##### Choke Hold:

This manoeuvre teaches the correct way of applying pressure to quickly render an opponent unconscious. The martial artist must make a successful attack roll to initiate the choke hold and must maintain the hold until the end of the next round. During this time the martial artist can take no other action; he is entirely occupied in holding his opponent. The opponent can attempt to escape on their initiative count by making a successful attack roll against the martial artist with a –2 penalty . The escape attempt can be tried as many times as the opponent has attacks for the round. Other characters may also attempt to free the choked character by making a successful attack roll, but if the martial artist is aware of them they suffer a -4 on their attack rolls as the martial artist uses the choked character as a human shield.

If the opponent fails to escape, he falls unconscious at the end of the second round and remains unconscious for 1d3 rounds. During the time the choke hold is in effect is unable to use their voice, the opponent cannot cry out for help nor cast spells requiring verbal components.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The attacker suffers no ill effects.

**Weapons Allowed**: Chain weapons, rope weapons, lash weapons, axes (using shafts), clubbing weapons, flails, karate weapons (using shafts), picks (using shafts), sickles, staves, bladed poles (using shafts), and spears (using shafts) tight groups.

##### Locking Block:

A locking block traps an opponent's weapon, arm, or leg in a scissor arm-lock. It can used against opponents using melee weapons or fighting unarmed and replaces one attack. The martial artist makes a normal attack roll and if successful causes no damage but the trapped element cannot be used to make attacks. In addition, so long as the opponent is trapped, the martial artist can make other attacks (using feet only) with a +4 to her chance to hit. The locking block can only be broken if the opponent succeeds on a normal attack roll (which also does no damage) or the martial artist is incapacitated.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: Used against an armed opponent, an unsuccessful locking block results in the martial artist being struck by the weapon, taking it's normal damage (not modified by strength of the wielder).

**Weapons Allowed:** medium blades, oriental blades, short blades, chain weapons, rope weapons, lash weapons, axes, clubbing weapons, flails, karate weapons, picks, sickles, staves, and spears tight groups. Characters using parrying weapons (parrying dagger, sai, jitte, etc.) receive a +2 bonus to hit with the locking block maneuver.

##### Incapacitate:

By gripping the opponent and twisting his joints, the martial artist can render one digit or limb useless for 24 hours and cause 1d4 hp damage in addition to his normal martial arts damage.

If the incapacitator's attack roll is successful, the manoeuvre does the damage noted above and the opponent must make a saving throw vs. paralyzation. If he fails the saving throw, the targeted digit or limb is rendered useless. If he makes the saving throw, the damage remains but there is no paralysis.

Application of a cure wounds or remove paralysis spell will eliminate the paralyzing effects of this attack. A spell used to restore use of the limb cannot simultaneously restore hit points.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The martial artist suffers no ill effects.

**Weapons Allowed**: chain weapons, rope weapons, lash weapons, clubbing weapons, karate weapons, staves, and spears (using shaft) tight groups.

##### Immobilize:

With one hand the martial artist grips and holds an opponent in such a way that the opponent cannot take any action except trying to escape. The martial artist is still able to make attacks (using any other body part but the single hand used), and can even attempt to immobilize a second opponent within reach. To initiate the immobilization, the martial artist must make a successful which does no damage. Immobilized characters can attempt to escape by using a melee attack to break the hold, however, with a –6 penalty on the attempt.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The martial artist suffers a –4 modifier on his next attack in this round, or the first attack in the following round.

**Weapons Allowed**: chain weapons, rope weapons, and lash weapons tight groups.

#### Principal Method: Movement

This method requires training to control and position the martial artist's own body— posture, stance, and reflexes. From this method, students learn the importance of speed, sudden changes of direction, and footwork. It is seldom very offensive, but when combined with other manoeuvres creates a dangerous and deadly fighter.

##### Feint:

The martial artist begins an attack in one direction and at the last moment changes it to another direction. Or he begins an attack but does not follow through, drawing his opponent off balance or making them commit to a futile defense. Performing a feint must be declared on the character's initiative count before an attack roll is made and expends one additional attack roll that the character has remaining in the round, granting a +3 bonus to hit on the current attack.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The martial artist's attack misses, but he suffers no other ill effects.

**Weapons Allowed**: Any.

##### Prone Fighting:

Prepared for any circumstance, the martial artist who knows this special manoeuvre is able to fight effectively from any stance: sitting, kneeling, or prone. This special manoeuvre is **always in effect** while using the martial arts style it was learned under and applies immediately any time the character is knocked down, falls, or adopts a stance that is not standing for any reason, voluntary or involuntary.

The character gains the normal AC bonus against missile weapons conferred by lower stances, but does not suffer any penalty to their martial arts attacks (unarmed or armed), and opponents do not gain the normal bonus to strike them with melee weapons. The martial artist also ignores weapon size limitations of a stance, but may not perform any other special martial arts manoeuvre (except instant stand) while in the lowered stance.

**Weapons Allowed**: Any. Fighters are even trained to use two-handed and long weapons effectively from positions on the ground.

##### Immovability:

By carefully positioning his feet and learning to tense and relax various muscles, the martial artist can avoid being knocked down, lifted, or otherwise taken off his feet. Any time an attack would result in one of these consequences, the character is automatically allowed a saving throw vs. paralyzation. If the save is successful, the character remains standing.

**Weapons Allowed**: Any. The character using immovability does not attack with these weapons, but instead uses them to brace himself. They confer no bonus to his immovability.

##### Leap:

The martial artist is able to perform extraordinary feats of springing and leaping by channelling his ch'i, or inner power, mentally making himself as light as a feather.

On a character's initiative count, before any movement is taken or rolls are made, they can declare they are performing a leap - this may be done in conjunction with a martial arts attack (expending one additional attack remaining in the round) or separately from an attack (expending only the current attack).

The leap itself expends 5' of movement regardless of the actual distance leapt, and may be made either with a running start of at least 10 feet (2+ grid squares) of movement prior, or from a standing start (0 or 1 squares of movement). The maximum distance that may be leapt:

- From a standing start: 4 feet up and 3 feet forward, plus 1 foot/per experience level in each direction
    
- With a running start: 8 feet up and 10 feet forwards, plus 1 foot/per experience level in each direction.
    

If desired the character can execute a flip in midair in order to land in any desired facing. The leap and any change of facing made during the leap does not trigger opportunity attacks, but any other movement or attacks made during the same initiative count may trigger them as normal.  
When the leap is performed the martial artist must make a successful attack roll vs AC 10 to land on their feet.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The character leaps the full intended distance but falls to the ground prone upon landing.

**Weapons Allowed**: The character can _carry_ any weapon while using the leap manoeuvre.

##### Speed:

The martial artist has honed the ability to act with nearly superhuman speed and power for a brief, but potentially pivotal, moment. Once per day, the character can choose to make a dexterity check before initiative is rolled. For every four points by which she succeeds on the roll (rounded up), she receives one extra attack, which may be used with armed or unarmed martial arts attacks. All extra attacks from this manoeuvre must be used in the current round.

This special manoeuvre is draining. Once the battle is done, the martial artist is reduced to half normal movement until they complete one full turn of rest for each extra attack that was made.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The character does not gain any extra attacks, but after the battle is reduced to 1/4 movement until they complete one turn of rest for every four points by which she failed the roll

**Weapons Allowed**: Any.

##### Slow Resistance:

Having developed his body and disciplined his mind, the martial artist is automatically immune to slow effects. This condition is in effect **all the time** (not only while martial arts are being used) and does not have to be activated like a combat manoeuvre.

**Weapons Allowed**: Not applicable.

#### Principal Method: Push

Pushing is a soft form of combat, relying on the character's understanding of the forces of motion. Many pushing techniques turn the opponent's own force against him, deflecting his attack, throwing him off balance, or knocking him backward with a single touch. Learning this art takes great mental discipline but does not require great strength.

##### Concentrated Push:

The character focuses his inner energies into his hands, such that just a gentle push can send an opponent a great distance. On a successful attack roll, the opponent is knocked back 4 feet plus 1 foot per level of the martial artist. If the distance is greater than 7 feet, the opponent must make a successful saving throw vs. paralyzation to remain on his feet at the end of this movement. If the opponent hits a solid object before the end of the movement, he suffers damage as if he had fallen the same distance. Opponents trained in the immovability manoeuvre can resist being knocked back.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: For the remainder of the round, all opponents get a +2 bonus to their attack rolls against the martial artist.

**Weapons Allowed**: clubbing weapons, staves, bladed poles (using shaft), pole spears (using shaft), and spears (using shaft) tight groups.

##### Sticking Touch:

The martial artist is so attuned to her sense of touch that by lightly placing a hand on an opponent she can read that person's movements in the same instant as their own muscles react. A successful attack roll must be made to make contact, and this touch does no damage. As long as the martial artist remains in contact with that single opponent, she gains +2 on subsequent attack rolls against the opponent and, because she is able to feel the impending moves of her opponent before they happen, her Armour Class improves by 2 against all attacks by the opponent.

The touch can only be broken by the opponent moving in a way, or at a speed, beyond the abilities of the character, the martial artist being incapacitated, or choosing to release the opponent. For example, an opponent who knows the leap manoeuvre could use it to break contact. However, if both combatants know that manoeuvre, the martial artist can - if they have an attack remaining - automatically choose to leap at the same time as her opponent, keeping the contact unbroken, unless the opponent's leap is longer. Any effect that causes forced movement on only one character can also break the contact.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The touch does not "stick."

**Weapons Allowed**: None; this may only be attempted unarmed.

##### One Finger Push:

This skill requires long and difficult practice. It is rumoured that a student first learns to push a suspended heavy bell with the touch of a single finger, and then must spend months focused on decreasing the force of their touch and increasing the force of their ch'i. They have only mastered the ability when they are able to toll the bell without touching it all.

The One Finger push gives the martial artist the power of concentrated push without needing to touch the target. The character simply concentrates and points his finger at the opponent. The martial artist can affect a target at a range equal to 1 foot per experience level. This manoeuvre requires great concentration however and is consequently the only action that can be taken in the melee round regardless of the number of attacks the character can normally make. A normal attack roll must be made. If successful, the opponent is affected as if hit by a concentrated push.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The one finger has no effect.

**Weapons Allowed**: None; this is an unarmed manoeuvre only.

#### Principal Method: Strike

##### Iron Fist:

Through various toughening exercises, the martial artist hardens his hands so much that they feel like steel. If the style's principal attack uses hands, the character's base damage is increased to 1d6 hp for each strike with bare hands. If the style's principal attack is not hands, only the first bare handed strike in each melee round is increased to 1d6 base damage.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The blow does not land.

**Weapons Allowed**: Any melee weapon.

##### Crushing Blow:

The martial artist is able to shatter or break hard objects—wood, ceramics, and masonry, but not metal—with a blow of his hand. As a guide, the character can break about a half-inch of wood or a quarter-inch of brittle stone per experience level. The DM can modify this based on the shape, hardness, and age of the object. If used against a living target, the character adds damage equal to half his experience level (rounded down).

The crushing blow requires great concentration and is the only action the character can take in a round regardless of the number of attacks normally allowed. In all cases a normal attack roll must be made. The DM must determine the number needed to hit an object (assigning an AC based on its hardness).

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: If the martial artist attempts to strike a hard object (such as stone) and the attack roll fails, he seriously injures his hand, suffering damage equal to what he would do on a normal attack. The hand is unusable for 24 hours, even if healed before that time has elapsed.

**Weapons Allowed**: None.

##### Eagle Claw:

Through a combination of concentration, conditioning, and sheer will, the martial artist can summon immense crushing strength into his hands. On a successful attack roll, he can shatter objects (snap spear shafts, crush stones, etc.), crush metal items, and cause 1d8 hp damage (plus damage bonuses for Strength) on a strike. This ability uses two hands and as it requires great concentration is the only action the character can take during the round.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The attacker does not damage anything but through mastery of ch'i t also suffers no ill effects.

**Weapons Allowed**: None.

#### Principal Method: Throw

##### Fall:

The martial arts student learns to fall and roll correctly, taking the impact of the fall on the safest areas of the body. Once learned, this manoeuvre is **constantly in effect**. Thereafter, the character suffers only half normal damage from any fall (if conscious and able to move).

**Weapons Allowed**: The character can carry any weapon when performing this maneuver.

**Instant Stand**:  
The student learns how to gain her feet quickly, either by rolling up to a standing position or using an acrobatic jump. Normally, rising from prone to standing requires a full round, and rising from other stances impose penalties to initiative and attacks, but a character knowing this manoeuvre can instead expend an additional attack to regain her feet instantly when forced prone (knockdowns or other effects), or at the start of their initiative count, without incurring the normal penalties of changing stance.

**Weapons Allowed**: The character can carry any weapon when performing this.

##### Hurl:

The martial artist can attempt to pick up an opponent, relying more on strength than leverage, and throw him to the ground 1d4 feet away. The attack adds 1d4 hp to the normal damage of the martial arts style. The attacker must make a successful attack roll for the hurl manoeuvre to work.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The attacker loses all remaining attacks for the round and incurs a +5 penalty to initiative in the next round.

**Weapons Allowed**: None.

##### Great Throw:

Using leverage and the opponent's momentum, the martial artist is able to throw an enemy a great distance. When an enemy attacks the martial artist they may expend an attack they have remaining in the round to attempt to throw their attacker before they are able to land a blow.

The martial artist makes a normal attack roll. If the opponent was stationary or has only shifted positions, the character can throw him 1 foot per experience level of the martial artist. If the opponent has moved 10 feet or more in the current round, the distance is 3 feet plus 1 per experience level, and if the enemy was charging, the distance thrown is 6 feet plus 1.5 feet per experience level of the martial artist (rounded up). The opponent suffers double the base damage for the martial arts style (i.e. 2d4 instead of 1d4, etc).

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The opponent counters the move and knocks the martial artist off his feet; the character automatically loses initiative the next round.

**Weapons Allowed**: chain weapons, rope weapons, lash weapons, staves, bladed poles (using shaft), pole spears (using shaft), and spears (using shaft) tight groups.

#### Principal Method: Vital Area

This method relies on a blow for effect, much like a strike or kick. However, the strength of the blow is not as important as the location where it lands. The vital area method teaches the martial artist where and how to strike at the weak spots on his opponent's body—throat, ears, foot, nerve points, and other places.

Because the training requires a knowledge of anatomy, the special manoeuvres listed can be used against only human and humanoid opponents, unless the practitioner has gained a nonweapon proficiency related to the anatomy of a category of monsters (dragons, equines, etc.).

##### Pain Touch:

The martial artist can cause great pain in his opponent by simply be pressing his finger against specific points of the body. This can be done in place of a normal attack. The touch causes no damage, but if the attack roll is successful, the opponent feels as though he were on fire, suffering a -2 penalty to attack rolls and a +2 penalty to his AC for 1d3 rounds.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** There is no adverse effect on the opponent, and the attacker suffers no ill effects.

**Weapons Allowed:** Clubbing weapons tight group.

##### Stunning Touch:

With a light slap of the fingers in the correct place, the martial artist can stun her opponent. This can be done in place of a normal attack and causes no damage. The attacker must make a normal attack roll. If the attack is successful, the opponent is allowed a saving throw vs. paralyzation. An opponent who fails to save is stunned for 1d4 rounds, and unable to take any action.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** If the attack roll fails or the opponent makes the saving throw, the attack has no effect.

**Weapons Allowed:** Clubbing weapons tight group.

##### Paralyzing Touch:

By applying pressure on specific nerve junctions, the martial artist can paralyze her opponent, leaving him unable to move for 2d4 rounds. The character must make a normal attack roll, but the attack does no other damage. The opponent is allowed a saving throw vs. paralyzation at a –2 penalty.

**When the Attack Roll Fails:** If the attack fails or the opponent makes a successful saving throw there is no effect

**Weapons Allowed:** Clubbing weapons tight group.

##### Distance Death:

Also known as the "death touch," this ultimate skill of the vital area method resembles the one finger push, requiring the same great practice and concentration. To learn this manoeuvre, the martial artist must practice at a pool of water, driving his finger at the surface without touching it. As he does so, he concentrates on his ch'i power, trying to extend it from his fingertip. When he can hear the echo of his thrust rebound from the water, he has mastered the manoeuvre.

Distance death requires great concentration and is the only action the character can make during a melee round. It has a range of 1 foot per experience level of the character. The martial artist must make a normal attack roll. If the attack is successful, the character can choose to apply one of the following effects:

- **Pain:** The opponent is not allowed a saving throw. The effect is identical to pain touch.
    
- **Stunning**: The opponent is allowed a saving throw vs. paralyzation with a –2 penalty. The effect is identical to stunning touch.
    
- **Paralyzation**: The opponent is allowed a saving throw vs. paralyzation, with a –4 penalty. The effect is identical to paralyzing touch.
    
- **Double Damage**: The martial artist does two times the basic damage for his martial arts style. No saving throw is allowed.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: No special effects occur. The opponent takes no damage.

**Weapons Allowed:** None.

#### Principal Method: Weapon

The martial arts often include training in a number of standard and unusual weapons. The manoeuvres described below are necessarily armed martial arts attacks and are exclusive to practitioners of the martial arts.

##### Weapon Breaker:

Just as martial arts weapons are designed to catch and deflect the weapons of others, they are also capable of breaking them. The breaking action is a swift blow or twist with the weapon. The martial artist must be wielding a weapon that they are trained to use with their style and can use this technique only against melee weapons, not against unarmed or natural attacks.

The martial artist must make a normal attack roll against a target it threatens. If the roll is successful, the opponent's weapon is broken (except that magical weapons are unharmed). This manoeuvre does no damage to living creatures or monsters.

**When the Attack Roll Fails**: The weapon is unharmed.

**Weapons Allowed**: Any melee weapon.

##### Steel Cloth:

With this manoeuvre, the martial artist will rarely ever be without a weapon.

If the character has a 6–10 foot piece of cloth or a rope weapon of similar length, she can whirl it and snap it tight, keeping it in constant motion and giving it rigidity. While in the martial artist's hands the object is treated as a spear with the length determining what type it is. If it is 6–8 feet, it is a normal spear; above 8 feet, it is a long spear. Note that the steel cloth cannot be thrown. The instant it leaves the attacker's hands, it becomes a normal piece of cloth or rope weapon.

This manoeuvre is automatically successful, no roll needed, and replaces one weapon strike.

**Weapons Allowed**: One 6–10 foot length of cloth, or rope weapons tight group.

#### Mental and Physical Training

The following abilities reflect superb control over mind and body and are gained by the martial artist in the course of their training. They do not belong to the categories of principal methods and at least a few from this group are available to every martial arts style.

##### Meditation:

This ability allows the martial artist to enter a state in which he can focus and regain his energies. For each hour the character spends in uninterrupted meditation, he gets rest equivalent to two hours of sleep. While meditating, the character does not experience hunger, thirst, heat, and cold (though he can still take damage from heat- and cold-based attacks). He remains conscious and aware of his surroundings, and suffers no penalties on surprise or initiative die rolls.

##### All-Around Sight:

Training has made the martial artist more attuned to his immediate surroundings. He is able to detect opponents on all sides, provided they are not invisible, and does not suffer an effect or penalty arising from a rear attack by any detected opponent. This manoeuvre is constantly in effect.

##### Mental Resistance:

The mental exercises of the martial artist's training have strengthened her will. She receives a +2 on all saving throws against mental attacks, including charm, illusion, and hold spells. This ability is constantly in effect.

##### Ch'i Attacks:

With this ability, the martial artist channels his ch'i (life force energy) into his unarmed martial arts attacks, allowing him to hit monsters that could otherwise be hit only by magic. See table below. This ability is constantly in effect.

##### Blind-fighting:

Under his master's tutelage, the martial artist has trained to detect foes with senses other than sight, practising for long periods while blindfolded or in darkened rooms. The character suffers only a –1 penalty when fighting in darkness, when blinded, or when faced by invisible opponents. Rigorous physical training has toughened the martial artist's muscles to the strength of iron. The armour Class of the character is improved by 2, when he is not wearing any other type of armour.

This is perhaps the rarest of all the martial arts manoeuvres because it requires the utmost concentration and mental discipline. Each day the martial artist practices making her body feel lighter by focusing her inner energies on negating her own weight. With time the character eventually succeeds in overcoming all her weight and can levitate for a number of rounds equal to her experience level.

This manoeuvre requires one round of undisturbed concentration before it takes effect. Thereafter the character acts as a class A flyer with movement rate 15. While levitating she can take no action except movement and will fall to the ground if her concentration is broken by taking damage, forced movement, etc.. If the martial artist voluntarily ends levitation in contact with an elevated solid surface - a roof, a beam, tree, etc. she may use her next attack to make a martial arts attack against a target at ground level and within 15' without suffering falling damage, or expending movement. This attack parallels a charge on foot, gaining +2 bonus to hit and suffering the associated penalties if the target is set for a charge, but it does not incur the initiative or AC penalties. The character must rest (take no actions or movement) for one round before using this ability again.

## Initiative

The initiative roll determines _when_ characters act within a given combat round. Initiative is not a fixed order and changes from round to round - combat is chaotic and uncertain. A character never knows for certain if he will act before or after another nor what the exact state of the battlefield will be when he acts.

There are 3 methods of determining initiative order:

1. **Simple Group Initiative**  
    A single initiative roll for an entire side or group, with optional group-wide modifiers.
    
2. **Modified Group Initiative**  
    As above, but individuals apply modifiers based on their weapons, spells, or circumstances.
    
3. **Individual Initiative**  
    Each creature rolls separately, applying all relevant modifiers.

The DM may choose which of these three methods to use in any given round, but outside of mass combat, where simple group initiative is most appropriate, will generally have PCs roll individual initiative, and will use either individual or modified group initiative for DM controlled characters, depending on their number and similarity. Note that simple group initiative cannot be used in combination with the other methods.

The dice used for rolling initiative is 1d10 and the _lowest_ modified initiative acts first. Characters with the same initiative count are acting **simultaneously** and the results of their actions are applied _afterwards_. i.e. The wizard's fireball may kill the archers at that same moment he falls to their arrows.

#### Multiple Attacks and Initiative

There are two distinct cases of multiple attacks:

1. Multiple Weapons (including natural weapons)  
    These attacks are resolved on the same initiative count
    
2. Multiple attacks with same weapon  
    The first attack occurs on the initiative count.  
    Additional attacks occur after all combatants have made their primary attacks (i.e cycle through initiative order multiple times as needed)

#### Spellcasting and initiative

Casting times of spells may be listed with or without units of time given (e.g. rounds, hours, etc). If there is no unit of time given, the number given is the modifier to the initiative roll. If there _is_ a unit of time specified, no initiative roll is made - the spell instead takes effect at the end of the last round of it's casting time (so with a casting time of 1 rd. the _infravision_ spell would take effect at the end of the round during which it was cast)

## Mounted Combatants

Combat on horseback - or any other mount - is not just normal fighting with greater height and speed, it is a trade off of precision and flexibility for speed and shock. The mass and momentum of the mount and the need to guide and control a living creature through the chaos of battle change the very nature of the fight. What follows are some additional rules specific to using mounts in battle and how the combat options presented earlier in the chapter are altered when the thunder of hooves pounding earth echoes across the battlefield.

#### Mounts - Untrained vs Trained

Mounts not specifically trained for combat are easily spooked by the noise and confusion of battle. All attacks made by the rider while mounted on such a beast incur a -2 penalty. Further if an untrained animal takes damage or is startled by a surprising event (a fireball exploding nearby, etc.) the rider must make a riding proficiency check to keep control of their mount and individuals that lack that proficiency automatically fail. On a failed check, the animal panics and bolts in a somewhat random direction (more often in the direction it is already facing but it will not run directly at something it recognizes as a danger) at 1.5 times its movement rate for 1d4 rounds. If there is no direction for it to flee the animal rears and bucks uncontrollably until there is a path for it to flee or until it is brought under control by the rider. A character proficient in riding or animal handling may attempt a check to regain control of the animal at the end of each round. Lastly, untrained mounts cannot move perpendicular to their facing - a move to the left or right requires the animal to turn their facing ten move increasing the risk that an opportunity attack will be triggered.

Mounts that are trained for combat, such as warhorses, do not have any of the limitations or penalties described above.

### Fighting from the Saddle

The following changes to the normal rules of combat apply when mounted:

- While mounted a rider and their mount are considered to have the same facing and act on the same initiative count.
    
- Where a movement rate or size category are referenced in a rule, those of the mount are used except when determining a rider's reach.
    
- The rider's stance (mounted) is treated as equivalent to standing except that they cannot change to a different stance while they remain mounted.
    
- Running/Sprinting uses the (inferred) ability scores of the mount for checks, not the riders, but the rider is still restricted from taking any other actions (except a flying tackle) during the round as normal, as they are busy controlling the mount.
    
- A mounted combatant can execute a withdrawal by 'reining back' their mount, but can only do so at 1/4 of their movement rate.
    
- At the DM's discretion a rider's reach may be _decreased_ due to size of the mount and/or the riders position on it - For example, a rider perched in a howdah strapped to the back of a fully-grown elephant is unlikely to be able to strike a dismounted medium-sized target with their short sword.
    
- A rider threatens squares within reach to their flank in addition to those to their front, and their mount separately threatens squares as normal (note that a creature that can kick back like a horse also threatens to their rear when controlled by a rider)
    
- Due to the higher vantage afforded by being mounted the rider gains +1 to bonus to attack rolls against creatures of smaller-size (than the mount) and opponents on foot suffer a -1 penalty to attack the rider (but not the mount) with melee weapons.
    
- The following actions cannot be performed while mounted: pummeling, wrestling, martial arts, overbearing, shield rush, trap (and break) and charging (instead use mounted charges, detailed later in this section).
    
- The following actions cannot be performed while a mount is in motion: casting a spell, called shots, covering,

#### Archery from Mounts and Vehicles

Combining the extra mobility offered by mounts and animal-drawn vehicles with the superior range of bows and crossbows, mounted archers can be truly devastating particularly when directed at dismounted enemies that lack the means to attack at range - the archers gallop in, fire, then retreat back out of reach.

While mounted or riding archers may not use long bows or heavy crossbows, and composite long bows must be custom made (at twice the cost) for the purpose, all other known bows are viable options.

The movement of a mount, and the need to keep control of the creature or vehicle, does however affect the archer's ability to aim and reload: A character may choose to take no penalty to attacks, at the cost of all voluntary movement for the round. If the character accepts a -2 penalty on attacks in the round, they may move up to half the mount's normal movement rate. If the character accepts a -4 penalty on attacks in the round they may move their full movement, however their rate of fire also decreases by 1. As usual when a mount is moving at a full run or sprint, the rider is not able to make attacks.

#### Jousting

While historically jousting was almost entirely an entertainment performed at tournaments to win favour or fortune, or more rarely, to settle disputes, it is a useful short-hand for any situation where mounted opponents equipped with _piecing weapons of equivalent reach_, such as lances, attack each other.

When a joust occurs the attacks are considered simultaneous regardless of initiative rolls and occur on the earliest initiative count of the two characters. A character with multiple attacks per round may only strike with their (primary) weapon once in a jousting round, though they may in the turn the joust attacks are resolved declare they are using an additional attack to perform a block or a shield-punch. As an exception to the normal rules that blocks only apply to attacks _after_ their initiative, a block declared in these circumstances applies to the simultaneous attack. While jousting a successful block performed with a shield also has a chance of breaking the opponent's weapon - the blocking character rolls 1d6 and if the result is 1 or 2 they have managed to break the enemy's weapon, the remnants of which may only be used as a club.

While lances may be used in many ways, their design was optimized specifically for jousting, not only for inflicting damage but also for unhorsing opponents. See the _Being Unhorsed_ section below for details.

#### Being Unhorsed

A major hazard of mounted combat is the risk of suddenly and unexpectedly becoming dismounted. This can happen if the mount is killed, the rider is pulled from the saddle, or even when simply struck by a very solid blow.

In many cases a proficient rider has a chance to either resist being unhorsed or to at least land on their feet and avoid taking damage by succeeding on a riding proficiency check; a non-proficient character automatically fails. A failed check results in the target being knocked prone, suffering 1d3 points of damage (DM may adjust damage based on terrain, the specific mount, and the manner of unhorsing), and being unable to take any other actions in the round. This applies when:

- The mount is killed from under the rider
    
- A successful pull or trip attack is made against the mount or rider
    
- A natural 20 is rolled resulting in a successful attack against the rider by any character wielding a large melee weapon, or by a mounted opponent with a medium melee weapon
    
- A lance strike does 8 points of damage (after doubling) or more while jousting.

In cases where a rider is unable to move freely, such as when tackled by another rider, overborne by dismounted opponents, ensnared or entangled by nets, lassos, etc. they are unable to resist being unmounted, cannot control their fall, and follow the rules that describe those attacks.

### Specific Mounted Actions

#### Flying Tackle

A mounted character may attempt to tackle a mounted opponent to try and unhorse them. This action may be declared on a character's turn either when their action for the round was running/sprinting, or when they have declared an attack, before the attack roll is made, in which case it replaces the attack action.

The flying tackle is an unarmed attack and may trigger opportunity attacks as normal. The attempt begins with a normal attack roll. If this misses the attacker falls to the ground, prone and suffering damage. If the attack is successful, an opposed dexterity roll is then made. If the attacker wins the opposed roll, they have taken control of the mount and the target has been unhorsed, falling prone, and suffering damage. If the target wins the opposed roll, they maintain control of the mount, but the attacker has managed to find a handhold and is hanging on, feet dangling just above the ground, and on a subsequent round may either make one-handed attacks with a small weapon, or unarmed attacks against mount or rider. If both characters fail the opposed roll, both attacker and target fall prone and suffer damage.

The damage for a character that falls to the ground as a result is 1d3 multiplied by the speed multiplier of the attacker's mount.

#### Mounted Charges

Whereas a dismounted charge is an attempt to smash _into_ an enemy with greater force, a mounted charge is an attempt to smash _through_ the enemy, breaking up formations and scattering individuals. Resolving a mounted charge is more complex then other actions as the order in which the attacker and defenders act is determined by position and reach, and it is necessary to work though each step of the mounted charge's movement until either the defenders break the charge, the attacker breaks through (e.g. has no more enemies along their straight line path), or the mount's movement is expended.

As with a dismounted charge this manoeuvre begins with movement; this must be a straight line and a minimum of half the mount's movement rate must be expended before an enemy is reached or the attempt reverts to a normal mounted attack. The maximum movement during the charge is **one and one-half times** the mount's movement rate. The momentum of a mounted charge grants a **+2 bonus** to attack rolls made during the charge, and as noted in the Master Weapons Table, some weapons inflict double damage when used while charging. Unlike a dismounted charge however, it does not incur penalties to initiative nor to AC, though a defender set for a charge still inflicts double damage when they make a successful attack. Only melee weapons may be used by the attacker performing a mounted charge.

A mount that is charging may not use any of it's attacks, however while the charge continues and there is an enemy threatened by the rider, the rider may continue making one melee strike against a creature they threaten at each step (5' of movement), up to their total number of attacks plus half the number of attacks their mount would normally make (rounded up). As an exception to the normal rules for multiple attacks with the same weapon, all these attacks are resolved within a single initiative count (i.e. during the charge). Any attacks that are not completed before the charge ends are lost.

When a step of movement results in the charging mount entering a square occupied by a dismounted enemy of lesser size, that defender must save vs. paralyzation with a -4 penalty added for each difference in size category beyond the first. On a fail the creature is knocked prone and suffers 1d4 bludgeoning damage from being trampled by the mount. On a successful save the character keeps their feet but is forced into an adjacent square of their choice. If the mount attempts to enter a square occupied by a dismounted enemy of equal size, an opposed strength check is made. If the mount wins the defender is forced into an adjacent square of the defender's choosing while if the defender wins the mount is unable to enter the square and the charge is broken with the mount remaining in the square they were attempting to leave. The mount cannot attempt to enter a square occupied by an enemy of larger size.

Riding through a thicket of enemy is clearly not without risk: Subject to all the normal rules for opportunity attacks (maximum number per round, no more than one against the same target in a round, etc.) defenders gain an opportunity attack against _either_ the mount or the rider (defender's choice), immediately upon the charging mount _entering_ their threatened area. As given in the opportunity attack rules, an attack that results in a knock down or that is a successful critical hit automatically stops the charging character's movement, breaking the charge. In addition if a mount takes damage from an attack, the rider must make a riding proficiency check, or if not proficient a saving throw vs. paralyzation to keep control of the animal. A failure also results in the charge being broken.

## Aerial Combatants

While aerial combat bears similarities with combat on the ground, there are some very important differences. First, many creatures must be in constant motion to stay aloft; facing off toe-to-toe while airborne is rare, and more often combats are conducted in a series of passes similar to the jousting at a tournament. While speed and manoeuvrability can be decisive factors in ground battles, they are critical in the air. Second, attacks can come from every direction - including both above and below. Tactics that work on the ground often do little good.

### Rules of Flight

#### Manoeuvrability Classes

How quickly a creature can turn while flying is measured with a rank from A to E, with A being the best. As a general rule more manoeuvrability results in more attacks and more effective attacks.

- **Class A** - Creatures that can move through the air as easily as a normal person moves on the ground - they can turn any direction at will, stop and hover. This class includes most creatures from the elemental plane of Air, and some wingless creatures magically imbued with the power of flight. While aloft they gain a -2 bonus to initiative.
    
- **Class B** - The most manoeuvrable of natural creatures - like hummingbirds - fall in this class. They can turn sharply, as with Class A, they can stop and hover in the air. While in the air they gain a -1 bonus to initiative.
    
- **Class C** - Most normal birds and flying magical items fall in this class. They must maintain momentum by moving at least half their movement rate each round (some magical items are exempt this requirement). Harpies, gargoyles, and dragons are common examples of creatures that belong in this class.
    
- **Class D** - Class D flyers are slower to reach maximum speed and make wider turns than most normal birds. Like Class C they must move at least half their movement rate each round to stay airborne. While flying they suffer a +1 penalty to initiative. Class D creatures include Pegasi, pteranodons, and sphinxes.
    
- **Class E** - Large or clumsy flyers that are incapable of tight turns. As with class C and D they must move half their movement rate each round. They also suffer a +2 penalty to initiative.

Classes B though E may only change facing by **45 degrees at a time**, except that class B may make one 90 degree turn each round. Each change in facing must be preceded by a minimum amount of movement as indicated in the following table (as normal, round up)

|**Class**|**Turn Interval**|
|---|---|
|B|1/6 movement rate|
|C|1/4 movement rate|
|D|1/2 movement rate|

#### Levitation

Levitation is not true flight, and is generally limited to only upwards and downwards movement. If a levitating creature is able to move freely they are treated as class A flyers. If they are not able to move freely they are not assigned a manoeuvrability class.

#### Altitude

The exact altitude of a creature might be important, up to a certain point, for falling damage, but for the most part what matters for aerial combat is the relative position - above or below - of the combatants. Creatures can dive down at an enemy (the aerial equivalent of a charge), but not up, a rider on a flying mount will have more difficulty striking a target below them than above, etc. Keeping track of the exact altitude of each flyer at a given moment would require a lot of bookkeeping and angular math so instead altitude is abstracted into 5 bands, the lowest being at/on the ground (with all the non-flying creatures). Above this are Low, Medium, High, and Very High bands. The relative position of aerial combatants is then determined by which band they are in. A creature does not threaten, and cannot attack, a creature more than 1 band away from the one it occupies, regardless of weapon reach.

As part of voluntary movement, once per round and on the creature’s initiative, a flyer may **ascend or descend by 1 band**. Descending has no additional cost, but **voluntarily ascending requires expenditure of horizontal movement** according to the creature’s manoeuvrability class:
 
|**Maneuverability Class**|**Ascent Cost**|
|---|---|
|A|0|
|B|1 square (5 feet)|
|C|2 squares (10 feet)|
|D|3 squares (15 feet)|
|E|4 squares (20 feet)|

A creature ascending must move **at least as many squares horizontally as the ascent cost**. If it's total movement for the round is insufficient to meet this requirement, **all remaining movement must be used for horizontal movement**.

#### Drift

While aloft most creatures are contending with changing air currents and thermals, momentum, and of course the need to counter the effects of gravity - only the most agile flyers can reliably hold their altitude in combat conditions.

Drift is a form of forced movement caused by these effects and is determined during the resolution phase of each round. Class A flyers are not affected by drift, and Class B flyers only need to make checks in adverse conditions - storms, strong winds, magical turbulence, etc. Those of Class C or less must make a check each round, rolling 1d6 plus any applicable modifiers, to determine if, and how, they are affected.

|Modified Roll Result|Effect|
|---|---|
|3 or less|descend 1 altitude band|
|4-5|no change|
|6 or more|gain 1 altitude band|

The roll is affected by the following modifiers, and others the DM may decide apply in the circumstances, based on the actions the flyer took during the round that is ending:

- **Descended or Attacked a creature from above:** −1
    
- **Ascended or Attacked a creature from below:** +1
    
- **Carried a load heavier than normal:** −1
    
- **Suffered non-minor damage while flying:** −1 (DM to determine what qualifies as 'non-minor' for a given target)

In extreme circumstances (hurricanes, tornadoes, substantial magical effects) the DM may rule that all flying creatures (including class A flyers) suffer additional forced movement that is cumulative with the effect of drift described above. These extreme effects may also cause creatures to be reoriented in excess of their turning radius.

If drift would push a creature above the very high altitude band it has no effect.

#### Crash Landing

Crash landings occur when forced movement would place a creature that is flying below ground level, or when it is unable to to avoid a fixed obstacle (for example a canyon wall) through turning, changing altitude, or landing.

When a creature crashes it takes 1d6 for each altitude band it descended during that round (to a maximum of 3d6), is knocked prone, and is unable to fly until it spends a full round regaining balance and control. This requires the creature to take no actions other than movement - recovering from prone to standing is considered part of this recovery. The creature may fly again on the subsequent round provided it is not prevented from doing so.

#### Falling

If an effect causes a flyer or rider to fall, the distance of the fall is determined by the altitude band they occupied at the moment of the fall.

- Band 1 (At/Near Ground) - 1d6 damage representing the combination of height fallen and momentum.
    
- Band 2 (Low) - The falling distance is determined by rolling 1d20; the result is the number of d6 rolled for falling damage.
    
- Band 3, 4, and 5 (Medium, High, and Very High) - The creature takes the maximum falling damage of 20d6.
    
Note that for most purposes the upper and lower altitudes of a band are abstract, not fixed heights. The assignment of falling damage to the different bands is a simplification and should not be used to infer heights for other purposes.

### Airborne Combat

There are two forms that combat in the air commonly takes. The first is more-or-less normal melee which is available only to creatures that can hover (Class A and B flyers as well as creatures that levitate but are not assigned a manoeuvrability class), and the second and more common form are attack runs which resemble tournament jousting and is available to all creatures with a manoeuvrability class.

#### Aerial Melee

Aerial melee operates by the same rules as ground combat except that:

- Stances are not applicable while a creature is in the air.
    
- Running (and sprinting) is replaced with Bounding (described later)
    
- Creatures must be in the same or adjacent altitude band(s) to threaten or engage in melee
    
- A character in the air cannot benefit from being 'set for a charge'
    
- Charging is replaced with Diving (described later)
    
- Disarms, barring some magical effect, result in the weapon or object falling to the ground. Depending on the item disarmed and the terrain below, retrieving it may not be possible while combat continues.
    
- A creature cannot sap a target in a higher altitude band
    
- If a critical hit results in a specific injury, the location of the injury is always rolled with 1d6 if the attacker was in a lower altitude band than the target, and 1d6+4 if the attacker was in a higher band.
    
- Natural weapons that the DM deems are required for flight (usually wings, sometimes tails) may be used while flying however impose an additional -1 modifier to any drift check the creature must make.  
    In addition, due to the limits of their movement (not able to fly backwards, do have a turning radius limit) Class B flyers:
    
- Cannot execute a withdrawal
    
- May only change facing once per round while in melee. Normal movement and attack runs use the standard turning rules given in the previous section.

#### Attack Runs

The majority of flying creatures are not able to stop mid-air - their flight requires near-constant forward momentum. Consequently they do not 'stand and fight' and instead, like jousters at a tournament, have one brief opening to make their attack(s) before they are past the target. To attack the same target again they must wheel around completely and make another attempt. If they are fortunate, and have multiple attacks, they may be able to attack a series of targets within their path, but are typically constrained in this by their ability to turn. It is perhaps worth restating here that movement is normally treated as though it occurs instantaneously while the character has initiative to act (see Dividing Movement section earlier in this chapter). This simplification obviously impacts on the 'realism' of attack runs, particularly where and when the paths two combatants in motion in 3 dimensions intersect - remember the rules are abstraction, not simulation.

Despite the similarities to jousting, attack runs use distinct rules, as well as being subject to all the limits already given for aerial melee. There are 2 distinct cases within an attack run that must be handled:

a. a target that is focused on a different creature, and  
b. a target that is focused on the attacker.

Which applies will need to be determined by the DM based on any actions already taken in the round and with consideration to what actions were declared before initiative was rolled. A character who has already made an attack, but not felled their opponent is clearly focused on a different creature, conversely where each creature has specifically declared an attack on the other it is clear. Between these two extremes there is need for DM adjudication and player honesty to decide which applies.

When a flying creature makes an attack run they attack on their initiative count and may make one attack sequence as they pass their target. If they have multiple attacks with the same weapon (more often applicable to riders on flying mounts) the person controlling the creature (DM or player), before the first attack roll is made, may choose between making a slow pass (following the normal rules for multiple attacks with the same weapon and dividing movement) or a blitz pass (all attacks - one attack sequence per target - are resolved on their initial initiative count). The latter option is assumed if not stated as it is more often advantageous for the flying creature - a slow pass often exposes them to more risk than blitzing enemies and flying off, and also potentially allows targets to get clear of the flyer's path of attack. A slow pass may however be desirable if more targets are expected to move into the flyer's path (for example if they must cross a bridge, or allies can force them into position.)

If a target is focused on a different creature, the attack is made following the normal rules for attacks. If however a target is focused on their attacker, the two opposing attacks occur on the same initiative count. The order in which these are resolved is determined by the following rules:  
For targets that are not flying: 1) If the target is set for a charge they attack first, otherwise 2) the creature with the greater reach, and if both are equal, 3) the flying creature.  
For targets that are also flying: 1.) The creature with the greater manoeuvrability attacks first, if both are equal 2) the creature with the greater reach, if both are equal 3) the creature with the faster weapon speed, and if these are equal the attacks are resolved simultaneously.

Attack runs trigger opportunity attacks as normal.

**Special Case: Extent-of-Reach Weapons**

Certain weapons, most notably lances, are designed to deliver a single devastating strike at the moment of closure and can only inflict damage at the extent of their reach. When two flying creatures are engaged in an attack run against each other, attacks made with such weapons are resolved **before** those determined by manoeuvrability, regardless of manoeuvrability class. If both combatants are using extent-of-reach weapons, resolve attacks using the normal order. Other reach weapons do not receive this priority and are resolved according to manoeuvrability as normal.

#### Diving

Diving uses descent to gain momentum and is aerial combat's analogue to a charge in ground combat. To perform a dive attack, a creature must declare the intention **before initiative is rolled**. At the start of their initiative count, the attacker must already be in a **higher altitude band than the target** (they may not ascend on their tur*n to meet this requirement) and must move at least **half of their movement rate horizontally** prior to the attack. If these conditions cannot be met, the dive reverts to a normal attack run.

While diving, the ability to change facing is reduced for all but Class A flyers, with the **turn interval doubled** until the attack is completed (i.e., if a creature normally requires 1 square of movement to change facing, they require 2 while diving). If the dive attack can be attempted, the attacker makes **one attack sequence** with **+2 to all attack rolls**. Claws and talons do double damage when used in a dive attack as do any weapons that do double damage when used in a charge.

A dive attack may also target a creature **two altitude bands** lower than the attacker, in which case a successful attack deals an **additional 1d4 damage**. The drift modifier for attacking from above is increased to **−2**. An **unsuccessful dive attack never triggers an attack of opportunity**.

#### Bounding

Bounding is the aerial equivalent of running, allowing a creature to achieve maximum horizontal movement to close distance rapidly, escape, or reposition. This comes at the cost of reduced control and prevents taking any offensive actions, except that a rider on a flying mount may risk a **flying tackle** (see mounted combat) while airborne, if they are confident—or reckless—enough to attempt it.

Like running, bounding must be declared **before initiative is rolled**, and the movement **may not be divided**. While bounding, a creature may move up to **1.5× its normal movement rate** for that round. Vertical movement remains limited to **1 altitude band per round**, and the normal horizontal movement cost for ascending still applies. For all but Class A flyers, the **turn interval is doubled** for the duration of the bounding movement.

Creatures with **Class C manoeuvrability or worse** must still move at least **half their normal movement rate** during the round to remain airborne; the additional distance gained from bounding does not reduce this requirement.

### Flying Mounts

A flying mount follows the normal rules of mounted combat while it remains on the ground, except that an untrained flying mount cannot be used for combat (either on the ground or in the air) - it will immediately take wing and flee to safety using bounding movement.

The following rules of mounted combat apply equally to flying mounts:

- While mounted a rider and their mount are considered to have the same facing and act on the same initiative count.
    
- Where a movement rate or size category are referenced in a rule, those of the mount are used except when determining a rider's reach.
    
- The rider's stance (mounted) is treated as equivalent to standing except that they cannot change to a different stance while they remain mounted.
    
- The following actions cannot be performed while mounted: pummeling, wrestling, martial arts, overbearing, shield rush, trap (and break) and charging (instead use diving detailed in this section).
    
- The following actions cannot be performed while a mount is in motion: casting a spell, called shots, covering,

The rules of jousting do not apply to a mount once airborne, and should instead follow the rules of attack runs. Similarly, while in the air diving replaces mounted charges. The rules for being unhorsed **do apply** to riders of flying mounts except that a rider who is _not_ using a saddle specifically designed for flying mounts (which sensibly strap the rider to the mount) and who is knocked from the saddle, or any rider who has their mount killed from under them, suffer damage as described in the falling part of this section. For riders that _are_ strapped in and knocked from the saddle, they are left dangling from their tether and incur the same penalties as being knocked prone. Recovering their position in the saddle is likewise treated as moving from prone to standing.

As alluded to earlier, a flying tackle may be attempted from a flying mount, but failure uses the damage detailed under falling in this section.

Note that when a flying creature is used as a mount, it's manoeuvrability is decreased by one class (except Class E which is the minimum). This reduction applies to all aspects of flight.

## Nautical Battles

The full rules of conducting ship-to-ship and fleet-to-fleet battles with full crew complements is beyond the scope of this chapter, however it is appropriate to give some general info to help guide players on how their character's fit into the nautical combat framework.

During battles at sea, _naval battle rounds_ are superimposed over ordinary melee rounds. The duration of a naval battle round is determined by the distance between opposing forces at the beginning of the round: at close range these may be the same one minute as a normal round, while at greater distances a naval battle round may encompass ten or even thirty minutes of in-game time.

Each naval battle round begins with a movement phase, during which ships manoeuvre to gain positional advantage (this is primarily resolved via opposed checks, not actual movement on the map) — closing with an enemy they intend to engage or opening distance from one they wish to evade. If opposing vessels are able to engage at the end of manoeuvre, they proceed to the action phase, during which ship-scale actions such as broadsides, ramming, and attempts to secure or foul an enemy vessel are resolved. The round concludes with a resolution of round phase, during which the results of these actions are applied and ongoing effects are resolved.

Player characters can influence the outcome of naval battles in a wide variety of ways. Some may serve as captains or officers, directing a vessel’s movement and actions, while others take on roles as navigators, gunnery leaders, boarding commanders, spellcasters, or specialists tasked with preparing the ship and crew for combat. Even characters without formal authority can materially affect the course of a naval engagement through timely action and preparation.

Although naval battle rounds may encompass many melee rounds, individual character actions are resolved using standard one-minute rounds. These actions occur while the ship is manoeuvring, the crew is making ready for battle, and conditions aboard ship are changing in anticipation of engagement. Depending on the current scale of the battle, this may grant characters significant time in which to prepare, reposition, coordinate the crew, or cast spells with longer casting times than would ordinarily be practical in a fight.

Characters capable of flight or rapid movement across or through water may attempt early engagements, raids, or sabotage actions before ships close. Other characters may use this time to bolster morale, clear and prepare the decks, repair damage, secure weapons and rigging, or otherwise influence the ship’s readiness. The actions of player characters commonly generate modifiers to ship checks and actions, and are resolved within the naval battle round before the ship’s own actions are adjudicated.

Once opposing vessels close to engagement distance, ship actions follow a more fixed sequence: missile and artillery fire, ramming attempts, efforts to secure contact or foul an enemy vessel, and finally boarding actions and boarding melee. Player characters act within the appropriate portion of this sequence. Those making missile attacks or hurling ranged spells will typically act during the opening exchanges, while characters leading or repelling boarders will find themselves drawn into brutal, close-quarters fighting as the engagement unfolds.

The Dungeon Master will often resolve these moments as small, focused encounters within the broader battle, concentrating on the actions of the player characters while the resolution of ship-scale actions provides a dynamic and often perilous backdrop to events unfolding around them.

## Combat in the Water

For typical ground-dwellers, the mysteries of the deep often remain such because of the extreme difficulty and danger operating in such an alien environment... adventurers are however seldom typical and will sometimes find themselves delving the depths in search of lost artifacts and untold riches, possibly coming into conflict with a civilization rumoured to lay on the ocean floor; other times they will have no interest in such pursuits but nonetheless find themselves dragged beneath the waves by the colossal and fearsome monsters that fuel the nightmares of sailors and fisherman. Combat in, and particularly _under_ the water presents a unique combination of challenges for any creature not native to that environment - many of these are detailed in the chapters _Vision and Light_ and _Time and Movement_, but to briefly recap the most relevant of those:

- Without another way of breathing, characters must hold their breath, which - under the exertion of combat - is likely only to last 1 or 2 rounds before they must begin making ability checks to continue.
    
- Wearing non-magical metal armour, being encumbered, or having movement significantly reduced for other reasons (injuries, etc) prevents swimming and forces characters to move along the bottom (which includes wading where the water is shallow enough) which is usually slower than swimming
    
- Characters who are not proficient in swimming _can_ swim (very slowly) but only in calm waters.
    
- Vision is extremely limited, even for those that can ordinarily see well in low-light on the land, reducing engagement distances
    
- At great depths land-dwellers must protect against the cold or suffer additional penalties to attack and damage
    
- Without magical assistance surface dwellers have difficulty communicating and coordinating under water as their ability to hear and speak are impacted, and limits on vision can interfere with non-verbal communication
    
- Without magical assistance, the pressure of travelling too deep can crush a character
    
- The exertion of prolonged swimming imposes cumulative penalties on attacks, and these penalties increase if swimming at faster than normal speeds.

Unlike aerial combat, where altitude is abstracted into bands for simplicity, depth must be tracked in feet, as the ability to surface for air can be of overriding importance.

#### Combat Effects of being Underwater

The conditions of fighting underwater are so foreign to land-dwellers that, in addition to any penalties and limits already imposed above, surface dwellers suffer a +4 penalty to initiative, and a -4 penalty to attack rolls when fighting underwater. Further the benefits of weapon specialization (attack and damage bonuses and any extra attacks) do not apply. The timing, leverage, and muscle memory developed for fighting on the surface has no benefit in the motion resistant and buoyant water.

Some magical items, such a ring of free action, spells, or weapons specially designed for use underwater can negate these penalties, but these are quite rare, and most surface dwellers are at a distinct disadvantage when fighting in the murky depths. Characters may also pursue proficiency in underwater combat to reduce the penalties and allow some benefits from specialization.

In addition:

- Only creatures that are walking on the bottom may be considered 'set for a charge'
    
- Water resistance greatly reduces the impact of weapons that must be swung - only weapons that are thrust are effective at all, and their damage is often less than when used above the water - use the weapon stats presented in the underwater weapons table instead of the master weapons table.
    
- Thrown weapons, except nets, do not work underwater, nor do missile weapons that are not specifically designed for such use. Missile weapons that _are_ designed for use underwater have subtle differences from the normal versions and require their own proficiencies to use without penalty, but characters that are proficient with the surface-equivalent suffer only half the normal nonproficiency penalty.

Marine creatures used as mounts generally follow the rules given in the mounted combat section except that a) any rule for adventuring and fighting underwater takes precedence over the rules in that section, b) falling damage does not apply - characters may sink if they were relying on the mount to carry their weight, and c) flying tackles and mounted charges are not valid actions underwater. Jousting is the most common manner of fighting on underwater mounts.

Spellcasting is also significantly affected underwater as will be detailed in the next chapter, _Magic_. To preview just some of the issues caster's face: verbal components suffer the same problems as communicating, somatic components may be as difficult to perform as swinging a weapon, and some material components may be destroyed underwater. Some spells behave very differently, or not at all, when cast underwater.

Even using magic items can be affected by being underwater - uncorking a normal potion bottle would contaminate or dilute its contents, dusts and powders are likewise unusable, scrolls are made of high quality paper and fine ink but these are not of themselves magical and are susceptible to water damage (as are most spellbooks), items with command words are affected by the limits on speaking, and like spells, the effects of magical items may operate differently in this environment.

## Special Defences

Combat is not merely making attacks, but also defending against them; in fact, avoiding damage is arguably the most important part of combat. Some parts of defence have already been introduced or alluded to elsewhere – wielding shields and wearing armour; using movement, positioning, and fighting in formations to avoid being flanked or attacked from the rear; making use of cover and concealment; using a character’s action to perform special manoeuvres such as blocks and parries. Spells are detailed later in this book and many have defensive uses. The most common defences not detailed elsewhere are saving throws, magic resistance, and turning undead.

### Saving Throws

Saving throws represent instinctive reactions on the part of a character – dropping to the ground to avoid a fireball, raising a shield to fend off an acid spray – the chance, however slim, that a creature avoids or lessens the damage taken.

How a successful or failed saving throw is described should be tailored to the events of the moment, but this description is merely added colour and has no bearing on the mechanics.

#### Saving Throw Categories

Effects that permit saving throws are normally assigned to one of the following categories:

- Paralyzation, Poison, and Death Magic (PPDM),
    
- Rods, Staffs, and Wands (RSW),
    
- Petrification or Polymorph (PP)
    
- Breath Weapons, or (BW)
    
- Spells (SP).

This is an ordered list and where more than one of the categories could apply the highest priority save should be used – for example a _wand of polymorph_ would use the RSW save rather than PP, while a polymorph spell uses the PP save rather than the one for SP.

**Paralyzation, Poison, and Death Magic**: This save is used for any attack that has a paralyzing effect, all strengths of poison, and any spell or item that can instantly kill a character (other than by HP loss). It is also used in any situation demanding exceptional will or fortitude.

**Rods, Staffs, and Wands:** In addition to the obvious usage implicit in its name, this save may be used to save against magical attacks from unusual sources.

**Petrification or Polymorph:** This save may be used anytime the character’s body must withstand a massive alteration, with petrification and polymorph being the two most well-known examples.

**Breath Weapons:** In addition to monster breath weapons, this save may be used where a combination of dexterity and stamina are critical to survival.

**Spells:** This is the fallback save, used against magical attacks from any source that do not belong to a higher category, and may also be used against any other attack that triggers a save but which cannot be categorized.

When characters attempt to use their abilities to react to a danger (such as diving out of the way of a falling boulder) the DM may allow an appropriate ability check to be made in lieu of a saving throw.

#### Rolling Saving Throws

A saving throw is rolled with 1d20. The result of the roll must be equal to or higher than the number given in the Character Saving Throws table for the character’s class group and level against the category of attack.

A character may voluntarily choose to not resist or react to a spell or other effect, in which case no saving throw is rolled. #### Modifying Saving Throws

Magical Items, specific abilities, and special situations may increase or decrease the chance of success. These **modifiers are applied to the roll result**, not to the saving throw number, consequently a positive modifier increases the chance of success, while a negative decreases it.

Some of the common sources of saving throw modifiers are:

**High Ability Scores:** Some abilities grant bonuses to saving throws against specific effects

**Magical Items:** Items like a _ring of protection_ may give a bonus to all saving throws, or against certain effects. Other items – particularly cursed ones – may instead impose penalties.

**Magical Armour:** An armour’s magical bonus is also applied to saving throws against effects that can cause physical damage that armour could reasonably be expected to mitigate. This never applies to gases, poisons, or spells and effects that affect the mind.

**Spells:** Spells may impose penalties against saves made to avoid their effects as given in the spell description.

**Poisons:** Weaker poisons often allow a bonus to saving throws to avoid their effects.

**Specific Locations:** Sanctified and desecrated ground, lairs, other planes and dimensions, and many other locations, may impose bonuses or penalties on saving throws.

### Magic Resistance

Some creatures possess an inherent resistance to magical effects. This resistance is thought to be one manifestation of an unusually strong cohesion (see The Weave); the creature’s essence is sufficiently unified that external magical forces have difficulty establishing themselves upon it.

This effect is not immunity, nor does it dispel or unravel magic that has already successfully taken hold. Like saving throws, magic resistance can voluntarily be suspended by the creature, and once this occurs it cannot be reversed even if the character was deceived as to the nature of the magic being used.

When a spell or other magical effect would directly affect a creature with magic resistance, the creature first makes a magic resistance check by rolling 1d100. If the roll is equal or less than the creature’s magic resistance, the creature successfully resists and is unaffected. If the roll fails, the magical effect affects the character normally, including any applicable saving throws.

Magic resistance only applies to a direct magical effect upon the creature with magic resistance. It does not stop the spell’s effect on other creatures targeted, or in the area of effect, and it does not prevent the _indirect_ consequences of a spell. For example, Magic Resistance may protect against the direct damage of a fireball spell, but not damage caused when the fire ignites a wooden roof and causes it to collapse. It does not protect against creatures, objects, or weapons merely because magic was involved in creating, summoning, or empowering them. If such a creature, object, or weapon subsequently produces a magical effect that directly affects the resistant creature, that effect is subject to magic resistance normally.

In the case of continuing or permanent effects such as _protection from evil_ or a permanent _wall of force,_ a successful check continues to apply while the creature remains in the area of effect. If the creature exits and subsequently re-enters the area of effect a new check is required.

#### Magic Resistance and Mysticism

Magic Resistance is related to, but entirely separate from, the ability to resist mystical powers and influence. Mystical effects do not trigger a magic resistance check and are instead resolved according to the rules for Mystic Integrity , Imposition, and Cohesion.

Under those rules, a creature with Magic Resistance of **50% or less** is considered to have a minimum **+1 Cohesion Modifier**, while a creature with Magic Resistance **greater than 50%** has a minimum **+2 Cohesion Modifier**.

This does not mean that all creatures with a high Cohesion Bonus possess Magic Resistance. Magic Resistance is only one possible expression of high cohesion.

Any spell, power, or item that reduces a magic-resistant creature's Cohesion Modifier also reduces its Magic Resistance. **For each point by which the creature's Cohesion Modifier is reduced, its current Magic Resistance is reduced by 25% of its current value.** This reduction is applied separately for each point of Cohesion lost. Thus, a creature with **80% Magic Resistance** that suffers a **2-point reduction to its Cohesion Modifier** has its Magic Resistance reduced first to 60%, and then to 45%.

Conversely, any spell, power, or item that grants or increases Magic Resistance may also increase the creature's Cohesion Modifier, if necessary, to meet the minimums given above.

These adjustments to Magic Resistance persist only for as long as the corresponding change in Cohesion persists. When the creature's Cohesion returns to its normal value, its Magic Resistance likewise returns to its normal value.

### Turning Undead

An important, and potentially life-saving defence possessed by many priests and paladins is the ability to turn undead, wherein they channel a portion of a deity’s power to cause undead to flee or, in some cases, be destroyed.

As a mortal vessel is limited in the divine power it can safely channel, a character may make only one turning attempt per encounter, and the effect is neither guaranteed nor absolute.

To make a turning attempt, a character must have their hands free except for their holy symbol, which they must hold aloft, and they must be able to speak words of condemnation. Only undead with line of sight to the holy symbol are affected. Unlike spells, a turning attempt does not require concentration; unless the character is incapacitated, the attempt is not interrupted by damage.

#### Making a Turning Attempt

A turning attempt begins by rolling **2d6**. The result is the number of undead the character can normally affect during the attempt. This is referred to as the character's **turning pool**.

Next, consult Table 70 using the column corresponding to the character's turning level. If several types of undead are present, begin with the first-listed type, or the type with the lowest Hit Dice, and proceed through the table in order. While the turning pool is greater than 0:

If the entry is **D**, a creature of that type is immediately destroyed. Subtract one from the turning pool for each creature of that type.

If the entry is **T**, a creature of that type is automatically turned. Subtract one from the turning pool for each creature of that type

If the entry is a number, roll 1d20. Only one roll is made during a turning attempt; the same result is used for all numerical entries encountered during that attempt. If the number in the table is less than the d20 result, a creature of that type is turned. Subtract one from the turning pool for each creature of that type.

Continue resolving undead in this manner until the turning pool is exhausted or there are no eligible undead remaining.

#### Bonus Turning

At turning level 8 or higher, certain types of undead are marked with an asterisk next to a **D** result. When such a result is encountered, roll **2d4** to establish a separate **bonus turning pool**. There is only one bonus pool for the entire turning attempt, not a separate pool for each type of undead marked with an asterisk.

While the number of undead remaining is greater than the character's normal turning pool, undead of a type marked with an asterisk are **turned rather than destroyed**. Each such creature is subtracted from the bonus turning pool rather than the normal turning pool.

Bonus turning ends when any of the following occurs:

- the number of undead remaining becomes equal to or less than the character's normal turning pool;
    
- the bonus turning pool is exhausted; or
    
- no undead of an asterisked type remain.

Once bonus turning ends, all remaining undead are resolved normally and affected creatures are subtracted from the normal turning pool.

## Injury, Recovery, and Death

The adventuring life is an especially risky one, and it is nearly inevitable that at some time—if not daily—a character will come to harm.

While there are some injuries which can affect a character’s abilities in more specific ways, most damage is handled abstractly through the use of hit points.

Hit points represent both physical resilience and the capacity to remain effective while wounded. The more hit points a character possesses, the greater the amount of physical harm they can endure before they are no longer capable of continuing.

When an injury is inflicted on a character, any damage rolled is subtracted from the character’s pool of hit points. This hit point loss is cumulative until the character either recovers hit points or dies.

Some effects may cause specific injuries that have other consequences, such as reducing movement, penalizing attacks, or lowering ability scores. These effects may be in addition to, or in place of, hit point damage, and may be temporary or permanent, though magic of sufficient power can often heal even injuries described as permanent.

#### Damage Multipliers

Some mechanics, such as critical hits, backstabs, or using a lance in a mounted charge, can multiply the damage of an attack. The multiplier applies only to the result of the damage dice; any modifiers are added after multiplication.

#### Temporary Damage

As described in the Non-Lethal Attacks section of this chapter, some attacks inflict temporary damage. Temporary damage still reduces a character’s cumulative hit point pool as normal, but the amount of temporary damage should be tracked separately, as it is recovered more quickly. If a character is reduced to 0 hit points by an attack that includes temporary damage, they are rendered unconscious rather than killed.

#### Falling

Adventurers have a peculiar penchant for falling, typically from great heights and onto hard surfaces. The rules do not attempt to model the physics of a falling object or the force of impact. Unless the DM rules otherwise—for example, allowing a _deliberate_ high dive into water—a character suffers **1d6 damage per 10 feet fallen**, to a maximum of 20d6.

If a fall occurs during aerial combat, refer to the rules in that section to determine the height.

### Special Damage

As mentioned above, hit point loss is not the only type of damage, though it is the most common. Critical hits, for example, have a small chance of being severe enough to disfigure, break bones, or sever limbs. These injuries have specific effects, often in addition to hit point loss.

Special damage effects are typically explained in the rules for whatever causes them, be it a spell, item, or ability, but a few types of special damage are enumerated here.

#### Paralysis

A creature that suffers paralysis is rendered completely immobile. It loses all voluntary motor function and cannot speak or move any part of its body. It can, however, still breathe, see, hear, and think. Any item, ability, or power that requires only coherent thought can still be triggered by a paralyzed creature.

Paralysis is especially devastating for flying creatures as most do not fly by will alone, and only stay aloft through movement. It can also be very dangerous for creatures in the water to lose the ability to swim. Even if they are capable of breathing underwater, sinking to too great a depth can still crush many creatures not adapted to that environment.

Even a creature such as a beholder, which can levitate at will and can still use most of its innate spell-like powers while paralyzed, is disadvantaged by paralysis: it cannot turn its eyestalks and must instead rotate its entire body toward a target, potentially exposing itself to opportunity attacks and attacks from the rear.

#### Energy Drain

Energy Drain, often an ability of powerful undead, is a particularly nasty power, as it can cause a character to lose one or more experience levels.

When a character is affected by an energy-draining creature, in addition to any hit point loss, the character loses one or more levels. This means losing the Hit Dice, hit points, and Constitution bonuses conferred by those levels. Also lost are any abilities granted by the lost levels, including proficiencies, and access to any spells and powers that are in excess of the character’s new level.

If the level or levels lost granted a fixed amount of hit points, those are subtracted from the character’s maximum hit points. If the hit points for the lost levels were rolled, the player rolls the appropriate die for the class and levels lost and subtracts the result from their maximum hit points.

Any prepared spells of a higher level than the character can cast at their new level are immediately forgotten and unavailable. Other prepared spells remain in memory even if they use more spell points than are available at the reduced level, but the caster cannot prepare any new spells until the total is less than their new spell point pool. All spells an arcane caster loses access to remain in their spellbook and/or arcane archive, but must be relearned once the caster regains the necessary levels.

A character’s experience points are reduced to the midpoint between the minimum experience required for their new level and the minimum experience required for the next level.

Multiclass and dual-class characters lose only one class level at a time, from whichever class is highest. If they are of equal level in each class, they lose a level from the class requiring the most experience first.

If a character is drained to 0th level, but their maximum hit points remain above 0, they lose all class abilities, cannot regain levels, and must retire from adventuring. The only way for the character to resume their adventuring career is through powerful magic, such as a _restoration_ or _wish_ spell.

If the character is subsequently affected by another energy drain, or if an energy drain reduces their maximum hit points to 0 or less (regardless of the level they have been reduced to), they are instantly slain. If the character’s former companions do not learn and perform the appropriate actions to prevent it, the character returns as an undead creature of the same type as the one that slew them after 2d4 days. They become a levelled NPC with half the experience the character had at the beginning of their last encounter. This creature’s goals are the opposite of those it had in life, and it holds particular hatred and contempt for its former comrades.

#### Poison

Few in the world, except perhaps cheating spouses and royalty, find themselves poisoned as frequently as adventurers. Perhaps this is the natural consequence of a life eating questionable meals in even-more-questionable taverns and inns, being bitten and stung by fell creatures, falling victim to traps, and gaining the enmity of the sort of villains that employ assassins.

There are many categories of poisons and venoms, not just one. Each may require a specific route of administration and have its own onset time, potency, effects, and duration.

The most common routes of poisoning are injection or ingestion, though some of the most dangerous and lethal poisons are inhaled or absorbed through the skin. Natural poisons used for defence by creatures and plants typically have shorter or even immediate onset times, while manufactured ones may have very lengthy onsets to allow the poisoner to avoid detection.

While a poison may have other specific effects, many common poisons are either paralytic or debilitating.

A **paralytic poison** leaves a character paralyzed for 2d6 hours. Their body is limp, making it difficult for companions to move them, but paralysis caused by poison is, on its own, unlikely to lead to the character’s death.

A **debilitating poison** weakens a character for 1d3 days, reducing all ability scores by half. In addition to the effect the reduced ability scores have on modifiers and rolls, the character’s movement is also reduced by half.

A character cannot recover hit points by either normal or magical means until the poison is neutralized or the duration of its effect runs its course.

### Recovery

If a character survives whatever has caused them injury, they will of course wish to recover. Characters can recover through simple rest and the natural healing of their wounds, through the care of a skilled healer, or through magical or mystical means.

The only general limit on healing is that a character cannot recover more hit points than their current maximum.

#### Natural Healing

Natural healing of lost hit points is a slow process. Assuming a character has adequate food, water, and sleep, they recover **1 hit point for each day of rest**.

A day of rest need not mean complete inactivity. Light activity such as travelling at a normal pace, riding a horse, researching, haggling with merchants, keeping watch, or mild carousing does not prevent natural healing. Strenuous activity, however—such as fighting, fleeing, moving excessively heavy objects, or repairing armour—is likely to strain or reopen wounds. A character who engages in such activity during the day recovers no hit points from natural healing that day.

Likewise, a character who does not have adequate food, water, or sleep cannot recover hit points naturally.

With **complete bed rest**, doing nothing beyond eating, sleeping, and occasionally attending to the necessities of life, a character recovers **3 hit points per day**. If a character remains undisturbed under complete bed rest for an entire week, they may also add their **Constitution bonus** to the 21 hit points recovered during that week.

Naturally recovering from specific injuries such as broken bones is an even lengthier process and can take months of bed rest. The most severe injuries may not be recoverable through natural healing at all.

#### Skilled Healers

Characters may receive care from a skilled healer, accelerating their natural recovery. The **Healing** proficiency provides the normal rules for such care, but NPCs, character kits, or other special rules may provide additional forms of non-magical healing or recovery.

Daily care from skilled healers reduces the time required to recover from specific injuries by half.

#### Magical and Mystical Healing

Magical and mystical healing offer a much speedier, often immediate, means of recovery, instantly closing wounds and restoring vigour. Only the most foolhardy or desperately poor adventuring parties would proceed without some means of magical healing at hand, whether through potions and other magical items or through magic-users with access to healing spells or powers.

Magical and mystical healing is independent of any natural healing that may occur during the same day.

There is no limit to the amount of magical healing that may be applied, except that any healing in excess of the character’s maximum hit point total is lost.

Curative magic and mystical powers may be used to heal specific injuries _instead of_ restoring hit points, but the strength of the effect must match or exceed the severity of the injury. An effect capable of restoring at least 5 hit points can heal injuries of minor severity; one capable of restoring at least 20 hit points can repair major injuries. Severe injuries can only be healed by higher-level magic, such as _regeneration_, while mortal injuries can only be repaired by the most powerful magics, such as _limited wish_ and _wish_.

#### Disease

Characters may acquire diseases in a number of ways including by being infected by some creature’s attacks. Diseases vary considerably in their symptoms, severity, onset, duration, and effect. For non-magical diseases, characters proficient in **Diagnostics** can identify diseases and their likely causes, while those proficient in **Healing** can assist in their treatment. In contrast, magical diseases may only be cured with appropriate magic.

### Character Death

When a character is reduced to 0 hit points, unless the final attack was explicitly made as a non-lethal attack, the character is immediately dead and unable to do anything unless a specific effect overrides this.

#### Death from Massive Damage

If a character suffers 50 or more points of damage from a **single attack** they must roll a saving throw vs. Death. If they fail, the injury was so traumatic that they are immediately reduced to 0 hit points and are dead.

#### Inescapable Death

Very rarely there may be situations where a character’s death is unavoidable no matter how many hit points they have. Triggering a particularly heinous trap protecting a powerful artifact might.

#### Death from Poison

Poison remains in a characters system for 2d6 hours after death. If the character is successfully raised from the dead in this time, and the poison has not been neutralized, the character must immediately roll a saving throw against the poison or suffer all the effects of the poison – depending on the poison this might immediately kill the character seconds after they have been returned to life.
