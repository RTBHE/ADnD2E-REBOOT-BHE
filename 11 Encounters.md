# Chapter 11: Encounters

An encounter occurs whenever a character comes into meaningful contact with a creature, object, location, or situation that demands interpretation or response. This includes meetings with NPCs, hostile or neutral creatures, environmental hazards, and unusual or unexplained phenomena.

Encounters are the primary means by which play advances. Encounters drive exploration, interaction, and conflict. They introduce uncertainty, creating opportunities for danger, reward, discovery, or negotiation.

The defining feature of an encounter is that its nature and intent are not fully known at the moment of discovery. Apparent hostility, friendliness, or neutrality may be misleading. Similarly, apparently inert situations may conceal threat or significance.

Characters must therefore rely on perception, judgment, and action to navigate encounters rather than assumption.

## Types of Encounters

Encounters fall into two broad categories: **planned encounters** and **random encounters**.

### Planned Encounters

Planned encounters are deliberately placed within an adventure. They are used to present key locations, narrative elements, or significant challenges. These encounters may involve negotiation, combat, exploration, or investigation, and are often structured to support the adventure and advance the story.

Planned encounters frequently provide access to important resources, clues, or objectives. Creatures or situations encountered in this way may be positioned as obstacles, guardians, or narrative drivers.

### Random Encounters

Random encounters occur when the character’s movement, activity, or time spent in an area triggers an unplanned event. These are determined by the DM using encounter procedures described elsewhere.

Random encounters typically represent ambient threats, patrols, wandering creatures, or incidental contact. They are less structured than planned encounters and often present immediate and limited choices: engage, avoid, or interact.

Such encounters may create risk, expend resources, delay progress, or alter the state of alertness in a region. They may also provide information or opportunity, but are not primarily designed to do so.

#### Non-Creature Encounters

Not all encounters involve creatures. A character may instead encounter objects, locations, or phenomena that do not respond in a conventional manner.

These encounters function as **interactive situations**. They may represent hazards, puzzles, or unexplained features of the environment. The correct response is not assumed; the character must determine appropriate interaction through observation, experimentation, or the use of abilities and magic.

Such encounters may conceal danger, provide benefit, or serve no immediate purpose beyond misdirection or foreshadowing. The outcome is determined by interaction rather than assumption.

## Encounter Procedure

When an encounter occurs, the DM resolves events in the following general order:

1. Determine whether either side detects the other.
    
2. Determine surprise if required.
    
3. Determine encounter distance if relevant.
    
4. Determine initial reaction where appropriate.
    
5. Allow the parties to act. (conversation, evasion, combat, etc.)
    
6. Check morale whenever circumstances require.
    
Not every encounter requires every step. The DM omits any procedure made unnecessary by the circumstances.

### Surprise

Not every encounter begins with both sides aware of one another. When one side is caught unprepared, they may be **surprised**.

The DM determines when a surprise roll is required. Surprise should only be checked when one or both sides are genuinely unaware of an approaching threat. If a creature has already been seen, heard, or otherwise detected before contact is made, surprise is normally impossible.

Surprise is normally determined individually. The DM may instead call for, or make, a single **1d10** roll for an entire group when all members are equally alert and exposed to the same circumstances. A result of **1–3** indicates that the creature or group making the roll is surprised.

The chance of being surprised may be modified by racial abilities, class features, proficiencies, magical effects, the character's actions, or other circumstances. Likewise, some abilities improve a creature's chance of surprising others.

Surprise is most commonly determined when combat begins, but may also occur whenever a previously undetected creature, hazard, or other threat suddenly becomes apparent during an encounter. A creature can only be surprised by a given threat once during an encounter.

The effects of surprise are described in the Combat chapter.

### Ambushes

An ambush is a deliberate attempt to attack an opponent before the intended victims become aware of the attack. It is distinct from ordinary surprise.

If the intended victims detect the ambush before it is sprung, the ambush fails, although a normal surprise roll may still be appropriate depending on the circumstances.

A successful ambush grants the attackers one round of actions before the defenders can respond. At the beginning of the following round, any required surprise rolls are made normally. If the defenders are also surprised, the attackers act before them again, potentially gaining two consecutive rounds of actions before the defenders can respond.

### Encounter Distance

Once an encounter has begun and any surprise determined, the DM determines the **encounter distance**: the distance separating the opposing creatures or parties when one or both become aware of the other.

Encounter distance depends on the circumstances of the encounter. Terrain, visibility, weather, lighting, noise, surprise, and the manner in which contact occurs may all influence the initial distance. Close terrain, darkness, confined spaces, and unexpected contact generally result in shorter encounter distances, while open terrain, good visibility, or advance warning usually increase it.

Encounter distance affects what actions are initially possible, including movement, missile fire, spellcasting, and attempts to avoid or negotiate an encounter. Vision and visibility are described in the **Vision, Light, and Detection** chapter.

## Encounter Options

Once an encounter has begun, there is no fixed sequence of outcomes. What follows depends on the nature of the encounter and the actions taken by those involved. Some common options are described below, but there is not a fixed set of permitted responses to an encounter, it is determined by the choices made and their consequences.

### Evasion

A group may attempt to avoid, escape, or disengage from an encounter. This is most common when a party believes it is outmatched, wishes to avoid risk, or seeks to conserve time and/or resources.

Evasion may involve stealth, withdrawal, deception, or outright flight. In some cases, it is immediate; in others, it develops into a pursuit.

Whether evasion succeeds depends on relative movement rates, terrain, visibility, and the actions of those being avoided. Some creatures or forces will pursue, others will not. A successful evasion ends the encounter; a failed attempt may instead escalate it into a chase or combat situation.

#### Pursuit

A pursuit occurs when one side attempts to escape while the other attempts to maintain contact.

During a pursuit, the distance between the two groups changes according to their relative movement, the terrain through which they travel, and any obstacles or actions that hinder either side. Faster creatures will usually gain on slower ones unless the fleeing group can exploit the terrain or otherwise delay their pursuers.

A pursuit ends when the fleeing group breaks contact, the pursuing group closes sufficiently to force a new encounter, or one side abandons the chase.

If contact is re-established, a new encounter begins. Surprise and encounter distance are determined according to the circumstances.

### Talk

Not all encounters require conflict. Communication may take the form of negotiation, interrogation, trade, deception, intimidation, or a simple exchange of information.

Talking may resolve an encounter entirely, defuse potential hostility, or provide information relevant to other outcomes. It may also aid or hinder later actions depending on what is revealed.

The outcome of communication depends on circumstance, intent, and the reactions of those involved.

### Fight

An encounter may escalate into combat when one or more parties chooses or is forced to resort to violence.

Combat should be understood as a consequence of the encounter, not its default state. The decision to fight carries risk, including loss of resources, escalation of opposition, and long-term consequences within the setting.

Not all encounters are best resolved through combat, and some are deliberately designed to discourage or punish indiscriminate violence.

### Wait

A group may choose to delay action in order to observe, assess intent, or allow the situation to develop.

Waiting represents a deliberate choice not to commit to immediate action. While this may provide information or prevent premature escalation, it may also concede initiative to the opposing side.

If waiting results in the opposing side initiating hostilities, the waiting group may be at a tactical disadvantage when combat begins.

## Non-Player Characters

A **non-player character (NPC)** is any person, creature, or monster whose actions are controlled by the DM rather than a player.

NPCs include everything from merchants, nobles, and common labourers to wandering monsters, powerful spellcasters, and legendary beings. An NPC is not defined by whether it is friendly or hostile; its behaviour depends on its goals, circumstances, and reaction to the characters.

Some NPCs may develop relationships with the party and accompany them for part or even all of an adventure. Such companions fall into three broad categories:

- **Hirelings**, who serve in return for wages or other agreed compensation.
    
- **Followers**, who accompany a character through loyalty, faith, reputation, or shared purpose.
    
- **Henchmen**, who adventure alongside the party as trusted long-term companions.

These relationships are governed by different expectations, obligations, and degrees of loyalty, and are described later in this chapter.

Many encounters, however, are with NPCs who have no existing relationship with the party. Such encounters may be resolved through conversation, negotiation, trade, intimidation, or combat. An NPC's initial attitude is not predetermined, and may change as the encounter develops.

### Reactions

Not all encounters begin with immediate hostility or cooperation. When creatures or NPCs first become aware of the characters, their initial response is not fixed.

Some may be friendly, curious, or open to communication. Others may be wary, indifferent, confused, or openly hostile. Many will fall somewhere between these extremes, and their behaviour may shift as the encounter develops.

The initial reaction of creatures and NPCs is determined by the DM based on circumstance, behaviour, prior events, and the nature of the encounter. In some cases this may be resolved informally through judgment; in others, the DM may use reaction rolls to introduce uncertainty and impartiality.

Player actions and behaviour can influence reactions. Displays of strength, caution, deception, generosity, aggression, or negotiation may all alter how a situation develops once contact is made. A character's abilities and proficiencies, as well as the effects of spells and magical items, may also influence an NPC's reaction.

Reactions are not permanent. As an encounter progresses, attitudes may change based on new information, perceived threat, or changing advantage.

### Morale

Not every creature fights until slain. Most intelligent beings, and many animals, have a limit to the danger, hardship, or loss they are willing to endure before attempting to withdraw, surrender, or flee.

Morale represents a creature's willingness to continue despite fear, injury, or deteriorating circumstances. A creature with strong morale may fight on against overwhelming odds, while one with poor morale may break after only minor losses.

A creature's morale is influenced by many factors, including its confidence, training, discipline, leadership, recent successes or failures, injuries, and the apparent strength of its opponents. Sudden reversals, the death of leaders, or unexpected displays of power may also affect morale.

Player characters act according to the decisions of their players and are not subject to the mechanics of morale. Allied NPCs, even the closest and most loyal henchmen, may however be affected

Morale is determined by the DM. In some situations it may be judged from the circumstances alone, while in others the DM may use morale checks or similar procedures to determine how creatures respond.

### Hirelings

A **hireling** is an NPC employed in return for agreed payment or material reward. Hirelings provide services rather than companionship, and their relationship with the employer is primarily contractual. They may be employed either by an individual character or by the party collectively, unlike followers and henchman who owe allegiance to a specific character rather than to the party as a whole.

Hirelings commonly include labourers, guides, porters, sailors, craftsmen, torchbearers, teamsters, mercenary soldiers, specialists, and other individuals possessing useful skills. Most have little interest in the objectives of an adventure beyond completing the work for which they have been engaged.

A hireling is expected to perform only those duties that were reasonably understood when employment began. Dangerous or exceptional tasks may require additional payment or may be refused entirely. Few ordinary hirelings willingly accompany adventurers into obvious peril without substantial compensation.

Payment does not guarantee obedience. Hirelings remain individuals with their own judgment, fears, and limits. They may resign their employment, refuse unreasonable orders, or flee if they believe continuing places them at unacceptable risk.

The availability, wages, and quality of hirelings depend upon settlement size, local prosperity, reputation, and current events.

### Followers

A **follower** accompanies a character because of shared beliefs, religious conviction, reputation, obligation, or similar motivations rather than simple payment.

Followers may be attracted through exceptional leadership, successful deeds, noble titles, religious authority, or other accomplishments recognized within the campaign world. Some class features or magical effects may also grant followers under specific circumstances.

Although followers often expect food, equipment, shelter, and a reasonable share of rewards, their continued service is not dependent on pay in the same manner as that of hirelings, and a follower is likely to tolerate a greater degree of shared hardship. Their loyalty depends primarily upon the relationship they maintain with the character they follow.

Followers are not mindless servants. They possess independent personalities and goals, and may refuse commands that violate their principles or expose them to unreasonable danger. Poor treatment, repeated failures of leadership, or betrayal may permanently end their service.

### Henchmen

A **henchman** is a trusted long-term companion who accompanies a player character as an active participant in adventures.

Unlike ordinary hirelings, a henchman not only tolerates hardships but accepts the dangers associated with adventuring. Unlike followers, the relationship is usually based upon a combination of loyalty, mutual benefit, trust, and shared experience developed over time. Many henchman begin as hirelings or followers

Henchmen normally possess character classes, gain experience, improve in ability, and expect an equitable share of treasure or other rewards appropriate to their contribution. Although they generally accept the authority of the character they serve, they remain independent individuals rather than possessions.

A wise employer treats henchmen with fairness and respect. Consistently poor leadership, unnecessary sacrifice, broken promises, or inequitable treatment may damage loyalty and eventually cause a henchman to leave or even become hostile.

Because of the trust placed in them, henchmen often undertake responsibilities unsuitable for ordinary hirelings, including independent missions, command of retainers, or the defence of strongholds.

### Loyalty

Loyalty influences how NPC companions respond when placed under pressure. Those whose loyalty is strong are more likely to remain steadfast in difficult circumstances, while those whose loyalty has been damaged may refuse dangerous orders, leave the character's service, or seek to end the relationship altogether.

Fair treatment, competent leadership, shared success, and the honouring of promises tend to strengthen loyalty. Conversely, unnecessary risk, broken agreements, poor leadership, repeated failure, or obvious disregard for an NPC's welfare may weaken it.

The DM determines the loyalty of NPC companions and how it changes during play, taking account of both the characters' actions and the circumstances of the campaign.

Characters who consistently honour their obligations, deal fairly with those in their service, and make reasonable efforts to protect or recover their companions develop a reputation as dependable leaders. Conversely, those who mistreat or abandon those who serve them may find it increasingly difficult to recruit and retain loyal companions.

### Obligations

Hiring, attracting, or retaining NPC companions is not one-sided. Hirelings, followers, and henchmen are not equipment or resources, but individuals who enter into a relationship with a character for specific reasons. In return, a character is expected to meet reasonable obligations associated with that service.

At minimum, this includes providing food, shelter, and basic care appropriate to the circumstances of the campaign. Equipment necessary for the role being asked of them is also expected, particularly where the work involves danger. It is unreasonable to expect a companion to risk their life without the means to do so effectively.

Beyond this, expectations vary according to the nature of the relationship and the risks involved. Those who are asked to travel, fight, or endure hardship will expect their losses to be acknowledged and made good where possible. A character who repeatedly allows companions to suffer unnecessary deprivation, neglect, or avoidable loss will find it harder to retain their service.

Companions who are injured, captured, or otherwise placed in jeopardy are not simply “lost resources.” Efforts to recover, ransom, rescue, or otherwise aid those in a character’s service are a significant factor in how that character is regarded by others in the future.

Spells, healing, and other magical aid are often used in this context. While such measures are frequently tactically advantageous, they also reinforce the expectation that those in a character’s service are not disposable.

### Reputation

The way a character treats those in their service is observed and remembered. Hirelings, followers, and potential henchmen share information, and reputations form over time based on patterns of behaviour rather than isolated events.

A character who is known to honour agreements, treat companions fairly, and make reasonable efforts to protect or recover those who serve them will find it easier to attract and retain capable NPCs.

Conversely, those who break promises, neglect obligations, or abandon companions in danger may find that fewer are willing to enter their service, and those who do are less likely to remain loyal when tested.

Reputation is not a formal measure, but an emergent consequence of action. It develops naturally through play and is interpreted by the DM based on events within the campaign.

### Recruitment

NPC companions do not automatically accompany a character by default. Those who enter a character’s service do so because they are sought out, persuaded, or attracted by circumstance.

Hirelings are most often recruited through direct negotiation in settlements where their skills are available. The availability and quality of such individuals depends on local conditions, demand, and the nature of the work being offered. More dangerous or demanding roles require greater incentive and are less readily filled.

Followers are not recruited in the same manner. They are drawn by reputation, notable deeds, influence, or association with a cause or tradition. A character may find that individuals begin to appear willing to accompany them as their renown grows, or as their actions align with particular beliefs or ambitions.

Henchmen typically begin as hirelings or followers who develop a deeper, long-term relationship with a character over time. Trust, shared experience, and proven reliability gradually transform an ordinary working arrangement into a more committed partnership.

Recruitment is therefore not a single act, but a process shaped by circumstance, reputation, and the character’s behaviour in the world. The DM determines when and where potential companions are available, and what is required to bring them into service.
